# Intervention evidence and translational design

An intervention can change a molecular target, alter a disease process, improve a tissue function, or change survival. These are related but different claims. Evidence becomes stronger when a study connects them with appropriate controls and when an independent experiment reproduces the result. A lower biomarker or longer lifespan can be important without identifying the mechanism, establishing better day-to-day function, or showing that the same intervention would benefit people.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:

1. Evaluate whether an intervention's mechanism is supported by its endpoints.
2. Design a study that separates efficacy from toxicity or selection effects.

## Match the claim to the endpoint

Start by writing the claim at the level the study actually measures. “The compound reduced target activity in this tissue” is a target-engagement claim. “The compound improved a pre-specified function in this animal model” is an efficacy claim for that model and endpoint. “The effect depends on the proposed target” is a mechanistic claim. “The treatment improves healthspan” requires a defensible set of functional outcomes over time, not only one molecular measurement. A claim about human health requires relevant human evidence; animal results alone cannot establish it.

These levels are not interchangeable. Target engagement shows that exposure changed a proposed target, if the assay is valid. It does not establish that the target caused the outcome. A functional benefit can occur through an unintended pathway. A survival difference can reflect delayed death from one disease, an effect on multiple aging processes, or another mechanism. Each possibility calls for a different follow-up experiment.

A useful evidence chain is: confirm exposure; demonstrate target engagement in the relevant tissue and time window; measure a pre-specified functional or survival outcome; test whether altering the proposed mechanism changes the effect; assess harms and competing outcomes; and replicate the result in a separate cohort or laboratory. Orthogonal perturbations, rescue experiments, and carefully designed interaction tests can strengthen a mechanism argument. None is automatically decisive: perturbations can have their own effects, and a rescue can be incomplete or act through another route.

## A mouse lifespan result is not a human treatment result

Harrison and colleagues reported that rapamycin begun late in life increased median and maximal lifespan in genetically heterogeneous mice of both sexes, with results observed at three test sites. The study is an example of a survival outcome in a defined mammalian model; it does not show that a measured marker mediates the lifespan result or that rapamycin extends human lifespan. The authors themselves discussed more than one possible explanation for the survival effect. [Harrison et al., “Rapamycin fed late in life extends lifespan in genetically heterogeneous mice” (2009)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19587680/).

To interpret a model study, identify the species and strain, age at treatment start, treatment assignment, exposure, follow-up, causes of death, and the exact survival statistic. Check how censoring and humane removals were handled. An intervention that changes one life-limiting disease may increase lifespan without broadly preserving every function. That can still be a meaningful model result, but it is a narrower conclusion than “the aging process was reversed.”

## Synthetic example: survival, function, and harm

Consider a synthetic randomized mouse study with 100 animals assigned to an intervention and 100 to control. By a fixed 24-month follow-up, 20 intervention animals and 30 control animals have died. The intervention arm also shows the intended target inhibited in 70% of sampled animals, compared with 5% of controls. Among survivors, the average motor score is 74 in the intervention arm and 75 in controls; 12 intervention animals and 4 control animals had a serious adverse event.

The fixed-time survival counts are 80/100 versus 70/100, an absolute difference of 10 percentage points in this invented dataset. They motivate a properly analyzed survival comparison, but the table alone does not account for when deaths occurred or whether some animals were censored. Target inhibition supports engagement in sampled tissue, not proof that the survival result depended on that target. The survivor-only motor averages do not establish preserved function: the animals available for testing may differ because survival and toxicity differed. The adverse-event imbalance also matters to the overall benefit-harm interpretation. These synthetic counts are a reasoning exercise, not published data or a treatment estimate.

Before seeing the data, a study team should specify the primary outcome, analysis time, censoring rules, functional measures, adverse events, and how missing observations will be handled. Randomize animals with allocation concealed where possible; blind assessors; balance or stratify important factors such as sex, litter, cage, and cohort; and report every randomized animal in the flow of the study. Power planning should match the endpoint: a study powered for survival may be underpowered for a functional subgroup or a sex-by-treatment interaction. Report uncertainty, not only a p-value or percent change.

## Test mechanism without overclaiming

Suppose the proposed mechanism is “the treatment extends survival because it suppresses pathway X in tissue Y.” A target-engagement assay can show that pathway X changed in tissue Y, but a parallel change in survival is still compatible with other explanations. A stronger design might compare treatment effects when pathway X is intact versus when it is selectively altered, with controls for the effect of the pathway alteration alone. A rescue or an independent way to perturb the same target can provide converging evidence. The design should measure the target in the relevant tissue and at a relevant time; a late terminal sample may miss the causal window.

Mechanism tests need appropriate controls. A genetic perturbation can affect development or baseline health, so inducible or tissue-restricted designs may be more informative. A drug can have off-target effects. If the treatment has no added effect when the target is absent, that is consistent with dependence but can also arise from a floor or ceiling in the assay. If restoring the target reverses the treatment effect, that strengthens the case only if the restoration itself is well controlled. State alternatives and the evidence that would distinguish them.

## Translate in steps

Translation is not a single leap from mouse to person. Ask whether the relevant biology is present in the target population, whether the exposure and target engagement can be achieved safely, whether the measured outcome matters to patients, and whether likely benefits outweigh harms over the necessary duration. Differences in lifespan, disease burden, immune biology, metabolism, and drug exposure can alter both efficacy and toxicity across species. A model can test a mechanism or generate a hypothesis; it cannot by itself establish clinical benefit.

Human studies also require endpoints matched to the intended claim. A biomarker can be useful for monitoring or stratification without being a validated surrogate for a clinical outcome. If the claim is improved mobility, cognition, or resilience, measure that function directly with a reliable protocol and an appropriate follow-up period. A short-term marker response should not be relabeled as longer healthspan.

## Common reasoning errors

- Treating target engagement as proof that the target mediated an outcome.
- Equating lifespan extension in an animal model with slower aging or a human benefit.
- Analyzing only animals that survived or tolerated treatment and omitting randomized animals with harms.
- Selecting endpoints after looking at results or reporting only the most favorable subgroup.
- Calling a marker change “healthspan” without measuring function.
- Treating replication in one laboratory or one strain as universal evidence.
