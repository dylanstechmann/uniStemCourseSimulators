## Part A: a distinct resource-limited pool

Begin with 20 ATP and eight NADPH available solely to the simplified carbon-assimilation ledger. Determine the maximum ensemble amount of net triose output, the CO2 consumed and remaining ATP. The reductant bound is eight/six=4/3 output units, compared with ATP bound twenty/nine. Therefore NADPH limits the output to 4/3, fixing four CO2 and consuming twelve ATP, leaving eight ATP.

Verify that the net carbon output equals the input carbon: four individual-carbon amount units become 4/3 three-carbon output units. Explain why a net triose output is not a net glucose output and why fractional amounts are valid on a macroscopic ensemble scale. If an export or product-synthesis cost is added, place it in the ledger before making a revised maximum-output claim.

## Part B: a distinct proton-coupling assumption

For twelve linear electrons, retain the foundation's ideal outputs of six NADPH, three oxygen molecules, 24 productive linear photons and 36 lumen protons. Now stipulate an effective cost of five protons per ATP. Producing the nine ATP needed for one net triose output requires 45 protons, leaving a deficit of nine. Suppose the chosen cyclic route contributes three protons and consumes one productive PSI photon per cyclic electron, without terminal NADPH or oxygen output.

Three cyclic electrons fill the nine-proton deficit, and the combined ideal minimum is 27 productive photons per net triose output. Show each contribution separately. Compare this with the foundation's different coupling and cyclic assumptions: the same final photon count can arise from different intermediate stoichiometric ledgers. Agreement in one aggregate output does not establish identical mechanisms.

If the linear route instead loses one sixth of its 36 generated protons before ATP synthesis while all other stipulated outputs stay fixed, useful linear protons fall to 30. The ATP target still needs 45, so five cyclic electrons are required under the separate three-proton route assumption, giving 29 total productive photons. State that this is a deliberately partitioned loss model, not a claim that real cyclic flux is unaffected by every leak or regulatory change.

## Part C: compare direction and compartments

Draw the mitochondrial and chloroplast electron sources, terminal sinks and proton-gradient directions. Mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation transfers reducing equivalents toward oxygen and uses the gradient to support ATP synthesis; oxygenic linear photosynthesis uses productive light excitations to move electrons from water toward terminal reducing power and releases oxygen. Both use chemiosmotic coupling, but sharing that principle does not make every carrier, compartment sign or yield interchangeable.

Explain why the Calvin cycle is not simply glycolysis reversed. Include the carboxylation, reduction and acceptor-regeneration roles and the distinction between ATP and reducing power. Identify what the simplified ledger omits about photorespiration, transport, light absorption, maintenance and cellular regulation. Link each omission to a specific output that could change rather than appending a generic caveat.

## Part D: failure and evidence analysis

Consider two hypothetical observations: oxygen evolution remains unchanged while net carbon output falls, or a supplied ATP pool rises while carbon output stays fixed. Provide at least two compatible explanations for each using the explicit ledgers. Propose an independent observable that could distinguish one pair of explanations. Do not infer a unique pathway block from one pooled metabolite or gas measurement.
