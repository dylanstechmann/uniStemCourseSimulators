# Cholesterol synthesis and lipoprotein transport

Cholesterol is a membrane component and a precursor for bile acids, steroid hormones and vitamin D. Because it is water-insoluble, the body moves it between tissues in lipoprotein particles. Cellular synthesis and circulating transport are linked by feedback: intracellular sterol availability changes both synthesis and uptake.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:

1. Trace the major stages of cholesterol synthesis and explain sterol feedback on HMG-CoA reductase and LDL-receptor expression.
2. Follow dietary and liver-derived lipoproteins, identifying the roles of ApoB-48, ApoB-100, ApoC-II, ApoE, lipoprotein lipase and the LDL receptor.
3. Calculate a synthetic hepatocyte cholesterol-pool balance from input and output rates and distinguish that cellular pool from circulating LDL concentration.

## Synthesis and feedback control

Cholesterol synthesis begins with cytosolic acetyl-CoA. Acetyl units form HMG-CoA, which HMG-CoA reductase (HMGCR), an endoplasmic-reticulum membrane enzyme, reduces to mevalonate using NADPH. Mevalonate is converted through activated isoprene units and squalene to lanosterol, then through later reactions to cholesterol. The pathway uses substantial reducing power and ATP; this lesson focuses on pathway logic rather than a single whole-pathway energy total.

Compartment helps distinguish two familiar HMG-CoA pathways. Cytosolic/ER-associated HMGCR supports sterol synthesis, while mitochondrial HMG-CoA metabolism supports ketone-body synthesis. Their intermediates share a name, but different compartments and enzyme sets direct the carbon toward different products.

HMGCR is a major regulated step. When endoplasmic-reticulum cholesterol is low, the SCAP-SREBP system permits SREBP2 processing; the active transcription factor enters the nucleus and increases expression of HMGCR and the LDL receptor (LDLR). This response can increase both endogenous synthesis capacity and uptake of circulating LDL. When ER cholesterol is abundant, SREBP2 remains inactive in the ER, reducing expression of those genes. Sterols also promote HMGCR turnover. Phosphorylation and hormonal state add further regulation, so gene expression is one layer rather than a complete explanation of pathway flux.

Statins inhibit HMGCR. In a simplified cellular model, this lowers hepatic cholesterol availability, increases SREBP-driven LDLR expression and can increase receptor-mediated removal of LDL from blood. The feedback response does not mean HMGCR inhibition has disappeared: enzyme activity remains constrained while receptor abundance changes uptake. This is a mechanism example, not a dosing or treatment recommendation.

## Lipoprotein routes

Lipoproteins place hydrophobic triacylglycerols and cholesteryl esters in a core, with phospholipids, free cholesterol and apolipoproteins at the water-facing surface. Apolipoproteins help assemble particles, activate enzymes or provide receptor-recognition signals.

The dietary route starts with intestinal chylomicrons containing ApoB-48. In circulation, ApoC-II activates lipoprotein lipase (LPL), which hydrolyzes core triacylglycerol and releases fatty acids for tissue uptake. The smaller chylomicron remnants retain dietary cholesterol and are taken up by the liver through ApoE-dependent remnant recognition.

The liver exports triacylglycerol in VLDL particles containing ApoB-100. LPL-mediated triacylglycerol removal converts VLDL toward IDL. Some IDL is cleared by the liver; remaining particles lose more triacylglycerol and become cholesterol-rich LDL. ApoB-100 on LDL binds LDLR, triggering receptor-mediated endocytosis. The particle is degraded and cholesterol enters the cell, while LDLR can recycle to the cell surface.

HDL participates in reverse cholesterol transport. ApoA-I-containing HDL can accept cholesterol from peripheral cells; LCAT esterifies surface cholesterol so it can be carried in the particle core. HDL cholesterol can reach the liver by selective transfer through SR-BI or through exchange with other lipoproteins. These routes are simplified: particle composition and flux vary by tissue and physiological state, and a blood cholesterol concentration is a pool measurement rather than a direct readout of any one reaction rate.

## Keep the cell pool and plasma particles in separate ledgers

For a defined hepatocyte pool and time interval, a simple balance is **change in cellular cholesterol = synthesis + uptake − conversion − export**. In this boundary, synthesis and LDL-receptor-mediated uptake add cholesterol to the cell pool; conversion to bile acids and export in VLDL remove it. The equation is a bookkeeping model, not a claim that these are the only routes in every cell. A plasma LDL concentration is a different pool: it reflects LDL production and remodeling as well as clearance by receptor-mediated uptake. A measured cellular balance does not, by itself, quantify the plasma LDL change.

## Worked example

**Problem.** In a synthetic hepatocyte model, inhibition of HMGCR lowers ER cholesterol. Predict the direction of SREBP2 processing, HMGCR expression, LDLR expression and LDL uptake, while distinguishing the inhibited enzyme from the feedback response.

**Reasoning.** Low ER cholesterol permits more SREBP2 processing, so transcription of both HMGCR and LDLR rises. The inhibitor still suppresses HMGCR activity, but increased LDLR abundance can raise receptor-mediated LDL uptake. Thus synthesis capacity and circulating-particle clearance respond through connected but distinct mechanisms.

**Synthetic pool-balance example.** Over a modeled interval, a hepatocyte has cholesterol synthesis of 36 mg/h and LDL-receptor uptake of 24 mg/h. Conversion to bile acids is 18 mg/h, and VLDL export is 12 mg/h. The net cellular pool change is **36 + 24 − 18 − 12 = +30 mg/h**. This tells us the modeled hepatocyte pool is rising at that instant if the stated rates persist; it does not mean plasma LDL falls by 30 mg/h or establish whole-body cholesterol balance.

## Common mistakes

- Confusing the mitochondrial HMG-CoA route to ketone bodies with cytosolic/ER-associated HMG-CoA reductase in sterol synthesis.
- Saying low cholesterol suppresses LDL-receptor expression; low ER sterol activates SREBP2 and usually increases LDLR transcription.
- Treating VLDL as an intestinal particle or chylomicrons as liver-derived.
- Mixing ApoC-II's activation of LPL with ApoE-mediated remnant recognition or ApoB-100 binding to LDLR.
- Assuming LDLR-mediated uptake and synthesis are the same process; feedback connects them but they have distinct steps.
- Treating a concentration or pool size as a direct measurement of pathway flux.
- Using the hepatocyte's cholesterol balance as if it were a balance for circulating LDL or the whole body.
