# Glycogen storage and mobilization: reciprocal control across tissues

Glycogen is a branched glucose reserve that can be built and mobilized quickly. Liver glycogen helps buffer blood glucose between meals; skeletal-muscle glycogen is used locally to support contraction. The same polymer therefore serves two different physiological roles. Following the carbon and phosphate ledger through synthesis and breakdown also explains why a glucose unit released from glycogen can enter muscle glycolysis at a different energetic cost from free blood glucose. This lesson uses a simplified mammalian model and is not medical or nutritional advice.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:

1. Trace glucose into glycogen and glycogen residues into glucose 6-phosphate, including the nucleotide cost and the roles of phosphorylase and the debranching enzyme.
2. Predict reciprocal glycogen synthesis and breakdown responses and distinguish the systemic role of liver glycogen from the local role of muscle glycogen.

## Build the polymer from an activated donor

After glucose enters a cell, hexokinase or hepatic glucokinase uses ATP to form glucose 6-phosphate. Phosphoglucomutase rearranges the phosphate to glucose 1-phosphate. UDP-glucose pyrophosphorylase then combines glucose 1-phosphate with UTP to make UDP-glucose and pyrophosphate; hydrolysis of pyrophosphate helps drive the reaction. Glycogen synthase transfers the glucose from UDP-glucose to a nonreducing end, forming an alpha-1,4 linkage and releasing UDP. A branching enzyme creates alpha-1,6 branch points; glycogenin provides the primer for a new particle.

For each glucose residue stored from free glucose, count one ATP at the hexokinase step and one UTP to activate glucose 1-phosphate. UDP is recycled to UTP using ATP, so that activation costs one additional ATP equivalent. The total is **two ATP equivalents per residue incorporated**, before counting any cost of making the starting glucose. Keep the nucleotide activation step separate from the glycosidic-bond formation catalyzed by glycogen synthase.

## Mobilize glycogen without first making free glucose

Glycogen phosphorylase uses inorganic phosphate to cleave alpha-1,4 bonds at nonreducing ends, releasing glucose 1-phosphate. It stops close to a branch; the debranching enzyme transfers a short segment and hydrolyzes the remaining alpha-1,6-linked residue, releasing a small amount of free glucose. Phosphoglucomutase converts glucose 1-phosphate to glucose 6-phosphate. In skeletal muscle, that glucose 6-phosphate enters glycolysis without a hexokinase investment. For a linear run of residues metabolized to lactate, each glycogen-derived glucosyl residue therefore yields 3 ATP net, compared with 2 ATP net from free glucose; branch-point glucose is an exception to this simple count.

Liver can convert glucose 6-phosphate to free glucose using glucose 6-phosphatase and release it to the circulation. Skeletal muscle lacks this glucose-export step, so its glycogen chiefly fuels its own work. A muscle cell can release lactate or other metabolites, but it does not directly export free glucose from its glycogen stores in the same way as liver.

## Reciprocal control follows phosphorylation state and tissue demand

In the simplified hormonal switch, insulin signaling promotes dephosphorylation: glycogen synthase becomes more active and glycogen phosphorylase less active, favoring storage. In liver, glucagon during fasting and epinephrine during acute demand can raise cAMP and activate protein kinase A. The cascade activates phosphorylase kinase, which phosphorylates and activates glycogen phosphorylase; phosphorylation also shifts glycogen synthase toward its less-active form. This coordinated switch favors breakdown and limits futile cycling. The details depend on tissue, signal, and enzyme isoform.

Skeletal muscle has additional local control. Contraction-associated calcium can activate phosphorylase kinase, and AMP can allosterically activate muscle glycogen phosphorylase when energy charge falls. ATP and glucose 6-phosphate signal a more energy-replete state and oppose some breakdown responses; glucose 6-phosphate also supports glycogen synthase activity. Glucagon is a major hepatic signal but does not act as the same direct glycogen-mobilizing signal in skeletal muscle. Hormones and local demand should not be collapsed into one universal on/off rule.

## Worked example

**Problem.** A synthetic, unbranched segment releases 4 mol of glucosyl residues from muscle glycogen and all of the resulting glucose 6-phosphate is converted to lactate. How much ATP is produced net by glycolysis?

**Step 1: identify the entry point.** Phosphorylase releases glucose 1-phosphate, which becomes glucose 6-phosphate without spending ATP at hexokinase.

**Step 2: use the glycogen-residue yield.** Each residue in this unbranched accounting case yields 3 mol ATP net to lactate. Therefore, 4 × 3 = **12 mol ATP**. A residue released from a branch point is free glucose and would require its own phosphorylation investment; the example excludes branch points by design.

For comparison, storing 4 mol free glucose as residues costs 8 mol ATP equivalents under the stated boundary: 4 mol ATP at glucose phosphorylation plus 4 mol ATP equivalents to regenerate UTP for UDP-glucose formation. The storage and mobilization calculations use different boundaries and are not a claim about a whole-organism energy balance.

## Common mistakes

- Saying glycogen phosphorylase releases only free glucose; its main product is glucose 1-phosphate, while branch-point hydrolysis releases some free glucose.
- Forgetting the UTP activation cost or counting UTP as if its regeneration were free.
- Charging glycogen-derived muscle glucose 6-phosphate for the hexokinase step it bypassed.
- Treating muscle glycogen as a direct source of blood glucose.
- Applying glucagon’s hepatic role identically to skeletal muscle.
- Assuming phosphorylation activates every enzyme; it activates glycogen phosphorylase but generally shifts glycogen synthase toward its less-active form.
