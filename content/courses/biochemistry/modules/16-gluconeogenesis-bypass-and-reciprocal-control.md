# Gluconeogenesis: bypass reactions, energy cost and reciprocal control

Gluconeogenesis makes glucose from non-carbohydrate carbon when glucose is not readily available. It shares many reversible reactions with glycolysis, but it is not glycolysis run backward: three glycolytic steps are strongly favorable in cells and require different bypass reactions. The pathway also consumes energy. Understanding the bypasses prevents a common bookkeeping error—treating glucose synthesis as a free reversal—and connects carbon supply to the reciprocal control introduced in the glycolysis lesson. Values here use a simplified mammalian liver context and are not a medical or nutritional recommendation.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:

1. Explain the three gluconeogenic bypasses and calculate the ATP, GTP and NADH input needed to form glucose from pyruvate.
2. Interpret reciprocal regulation of glycolysis and gluconeogenesis and identify carbon sources that can or cannot support net glucose synthesis.

## Three bypasses replace the one-way steps

Most reactions between phosphoenolpyruvate (PEP) and fructose 1,6-bisphosphate can operate in either direction, depending on conditions. Three steps cannot simply be reversed at useful cellular rates, so gluconeogenesis uses different reactions:

1. **Pyruvate kinase bypass.** Mitochondrial pyruvate carboxylase uses ATP and bicarbonate to convert pyruvate to oxaloacetate. Phosphoenolpyruvate carboxykinase (PEPCK) uses GTP to convert oxaloacetate to PEP while releasing CO₂. These two reactions replace the ATP-producing PEP-to-pyruvate step of glycolysis. Oxaloacetate can be transferred between compartments indirectly as malate or aspartate; it does not freely cross the inner mitochondrial membrane.
2. **Phosphofructokinase-1 bypass.** Fructose 1,6-bisphosphatase hydrolyzes fructose 1,6-bisphosphate to fructose 6-phosphate and inorganic phosphate. This is not the reverse of ATP-driven PFK-1: it removes a phosphate rather than making ATP.
3. **Hexokinase/glucokinase bypass.** Glucose 6-phosphatase hydrolyzes glucose 6-phosphate to glucose and inorganic phosphate. This avoids reversing the strongly favorable phosphorylation of glucose. In humans, the liver and kidney are major sites of glucose production and release; most tissues use glucose 6-phosphate locally instead of exporting free glucose.

These separate reactions prevent a direct, wasteful cycle in which ATP is consumed by a kinase while the same substrate is immediately dephosphorylated. The bypasses have their own regulation and are not interchangeable with the glycolytic enzymes.

## Count the energy input

Starting from two pyruvate molecules, synthesis of one glucose requires **4 ATP, 2 GTP and 2 NADH**. The four ATP and two GTP supply six high-energy phosphate equivalents; the NADH supplies reducing power. This is an input cost, not an ATP yield. When lactate is the carbon source, lactate oxidation to pyruvate also makes NADH, so the redox ledger at the start differs from the pyruvate case. State the input substrate and the accounting boundary before comparing pathways.

Carbon sources enter at different points. Lactate and alanine can supply pyruvate; glycerol enters as a triose-phosphate intermediate. Many amino-acid carbon skeletons can contribute through pyruvate or cycle intermediates. In mammals, oxidation of even-chain fatty acids produces acetyl-CoA but no net glucose carbon: the cycle releases carbon as CO₂ and does not provide a net route from acetyl-CoA back to oxaloacetate. The glycerol portion of a triacylglycerol is a distinct glucose precursor; do not treat the fatty-acid chains and glycerol backbone as the same carbon source.

## Reciprocal regulation limits futile cycling

In liver, fructose 2,6-bisphosphate (F2,6BP) coordinates a key opposing pair. It activates phosphofructokinase-1, favoring glycolysis, and inhibits fructose 1,6-bisphosphatase, opposing gluconeogenesis. A higher F2,6BP concentration therefore favors use of glucose through glycolysis rather than simultaneous high flux through the opposing step. AMP also signals low energy availability: it activates PFK-1 and inhibits FBPase-1. In the liver isoform of the bifunctional PFK-2/FBPase-2 regulator, glucagon signaling lowers F2,6BP; the reciprocal enzyme effects then favor gluconeogenic direction. This is a tissue-specific regulatory example, not a rule that every cell responds identically.

Glycolysis and gluconeogenesis can both be active in a tissue because different cells, compartments or time periods contribute, but strong simultaneous flux through the same reversible segment would waste energy. F2,6BP and AMP help coordinate opposing control points; substrate supply, hormones and enzyme expression add other layers. A single metabolite concentration cannot establish whole-organ glucose output or flux.

## Worked example

**Problem.** A synthetic hepatic preparation converts 2.5 mol of pyruvate pairs to 2.5 mol glucose through gluconeogenesis. How much ATP, GTP and NADH does the stated pathway accounting require?

**Step 1: use the per-glucose inputs.** Each glucose from two pyruvate requires 4 mol ATP, 2 mol GTP and 2 mol NADH.

**Step 2: scale by 2.5.** ATP: 2.5 × 4 = **10 mol**. GTP: 2.5 × 2 = **5 mol**. NADH: 2.5 × 2 = **5 mol**.

The six high-energy phosphate equivalents per glucose are split between ATP and GTP; they are not six ATP molecules. The accounting also excludes the energy required to make the precursor pyruvate from other fuels.

## Common mistakes

- Reversing PFK-1 or pyruvate kinase directly instead of using the bypass reactions.
- Counting PEPCK’s GTP use but omitting the ATP used by pyruvate carboxylase.
- Treating glucose 6-phosphatase hydrolysis as an ATP-producing reaction.
- Saying gluconeogenesis costs only six ATP molecules instead of 4 ATP plus 2 GTP and 2 NADH from pyruvate.
- Claiming that even-chain fatty-acid acetyl-CoA supplies net glucose in mammals.
- Treating a liver regulatory example as a universal response of all tissues.
