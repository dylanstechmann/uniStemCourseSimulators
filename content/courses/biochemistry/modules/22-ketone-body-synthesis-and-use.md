# Ketone-body synthesis and use: a fasting fuel connection

Ketone bodies let the liver export a water-soluble form of acetyl-CoA-derived carbon. Their synthesis and use connect fatty-acid beta-oxidation, gluconeogenesis, the citric acid cycle and mitochondrial redox. In a simplified fasting model, hepatic fatty-acid oxidation supplies acetyl-CoA and NADH while gluconeogenesis competes for carbon intermediates. Extrahepatic mitochondria use ketones through the SCOT-dependent oxidative route. Mature hepatocytes generally lack that route, but this does not mean every liver cell or pathway is unable to handle ketone-derived carbon.

## Make and export ketone bodies in liver mitochondria

The three commonly named ketone bodies are acetoacetate, D-beta-hydroxybutyrate and acetone. In the liver mitochondrial matrix, high fatty-acid oxidation can supply acetyl-CoA for ketogenesis. Mitochondrial HMG-CoA synthase combines acetyl-CoA-derived intermediates into HMG-CoA; HMG-CoA lyase then forms acetoacetate and acetyl-CoA. Acetoacetate can be reduced to beta-hydroxybutyrate by beta-hydroxybutyrate dehydrogenase or can decarboxylate spontaneously to acetone. Beta-hydroxybutyrate is not chemically a ketone, but it is grouped with the ketone bodies by its metabolic relationship to acetoacetate.

The beta-hydroxybutyrate/acetoacetate ratio reflects the mitochondrial NADH/NAD+ state through the dehydrogenase reaction: a more reduced state favors beta-hydroxybutyrate. It is a redox-linked ratio, not a direct measurement of whole-body ATP production.

During fasting, fatty-acid delivery and beta-oxidation can increase while the liver also makes glucose. Oxaloacetate and other intermediates participate in gluconeogenesis; depending on supply and demand, this can limit how much acetyl-CoA is condensed into citrate and favor ketone-body production. This is a network-level connection, not a rule that oxaloacetate always becomes depleted or that the citric acid cycle stops.

## Use ketones in extrahepatic mitochondria

D-beta-hydroxybutyrate (D-BHB) is first oxidized to acetoacetate by mitochondrial beta-hydroxybutyrate dehydrogenase (BDH1), reducing NAD+ to NADH. SCOT/OXCT1 then transfers CoA from succinyl-CoA to acetoacetate, making acetoacetyl-CoA and succinate. Thiolase cleaves acetoacetyl-CoA into two acetyl-CoA molecules, which can enter the citric acid cycle. SCOT does not directly spend ATP, but it uses succinyl-CoA instead of letting succinyl-CoA synthetase make GTP at that step.

Tissues with mitochondria can oxidize ketones when transport, enzyme expression and metabolic conditions support it. Red blood cells cannot perform mitochondrial ketolysis because they lack mitochondria. These tissue distinctions describe pathway capacity; they do not say that every cell in a named tissue has identical enzyme levels or fuel use.

## Conditional ATP-equivalent accounting

A transparent estimate must state its convention. Use the course’s illustrative P/O values of 2.5 ATP per NADH and 1.5 per FADH2, count one GTP as one ATP-equivalent, assume complete oxidation of both acetyl-CoA molecules, and ignore transport, leak, maintenance and biosynthetic withdrawal. Two acetyl-CoA make 6 NADH, 2 FADH2 and 2 GTP in two cycle turns. The SCOT reaction consumes one succinyl-CoA and bypasses one GTP-forming succinyl-CoA synthetase step, leaving one net GTP-equivalent in this bookkeeping.

| Starting ketone, per mole | NADH counted | FADH2 | Net GTP-equivalents | Conditional ATP-equivalents |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Acetoacetate | 6 | 2 | 1 | 6(2.5) + 2(1.5) + 1 = **19** |
| D-BHB | 7 | 2 | 1 | 7(2.5) + 2(1.5) + 1 = **21.5** |

The extra NADH for D-BHB comes from its oxidation to acetoacetate. Thus, under this explicitly chosen convention, two millimoles of D-BHB correspond to 43 mmol ATP-equivalents if completely oxidized. This is a model estimate, not a fixed cellular yield: P/O ratios, proton leak, transport costs, tissue state, and diversion of acetyl-CoA change observed conservation. Do not interpret a blood ketone concentration or BHB/acetoacetate ratio as a direct ATP production rate.

## What does “liver does not use ketones” mean?

Human tissue measurements reported SCOT protein in several oxidative tissues but did not detect it in liver samples, supporting the standard distinction between hepatic ketogenesis and SCOT-dependent extrahepatic ketolysis. That evidence supports a pathway-specific claim about canonical oxidation; it does not prove that every liver cell lacks every route for ketone-carbon uptake or metabolism.

A 2025 stable-isotope study in mice and primary mouse hepatocytes reported ketone-derived carbon entering lipid synthesis, including polyunsaturated-fatty-acid elongation through an AACS-dependent route. This is SCOT-independent anabolic use, not evidence that hepatocytes perform the canonical SCOT-dependent oxidative route or obtain the ATP-equivalent yield in the table from those lipids. ## Worked example

**Problem.** A synthetic extrahepatic mitochondrial model completely oxidizes 2.0 mmol D-BHB. Use 2.5 ATP per NADH, 1.5 per FADH2, one GTP per cycle turn, and the SCOT bypass of one GTP-forming step. How many mmol ATP-equivalents does the model assign?

**Step 1: count cofactors.** Per mole D-BHB, the two acetyl-CoA yield 6 NADH and 2 FADH2; D-BHB oxidation adds 1 NADH. The two cycle turns produce 2 GTP, but SCOT bypasses one GTP-forming step, leaving 1 net GTP-equivalent.

**Step 2: convert to the stated convention.** Per mole: 7 × 2.5 + 2 × 1.5 + 1 = 21.5 ATP-equivalents.

**Step 3: scale the amount.** 2.0 mmol × 21.5 mmol ATP-equivalents per mmol D-BHB = **43 mmol ATP-equivalents**. This answer belongs only to the model and assumptions stated in the problem.

## Common mistakes

- Treating beta-hydroxybutyrate as chemically a ketone rather than a metabolically grouped ketone body.
- Assuming the liver oxidizes the ketone bodies it produces; it lacks SCOT for the canonical ketolysis step.
- Saying all tissues can use ketones; red blood cells lack mitochondria.
- Treating a high beta-hydroxybutyrate/acetoacetate ratio as an ATP-yield measurement rather than a redox-linked ratio.
- Forgetting that SCOT diverts succinyl-CoA from one GTP-forming step when making a conditional ATP-equivalent estimate.
- Claiming gluconeogenesis always shuts down the citric acid cycle or completely removes oxaloacetate.
- Confusing hepatic ketone-body synthesis with extrahepatic ketone-body utilization.
