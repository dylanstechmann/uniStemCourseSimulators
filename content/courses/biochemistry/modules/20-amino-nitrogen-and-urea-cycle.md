# Amino-acid nitrogen and the urea cycle

Amino-acid catabolism separates nitrogen handling from carbon-skeleton use. Transamination moves amino groups among amino acids and alpha-keto acids, often collecting nitrogen on glutamate. Oxidative deamination can release ammonium, while aspartate carries a second nitrogen into the hepatic urea cycle. The cycle converts these two nitrogen atoms and a bicarbonate-derived carbon into urea, regenerating ornithine for another turn. This lesson focuses on the atom and energy ledgers, not clinical diagnosis or dietary advice.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:

1. Trace the two nitrogen atoms and the carbon atom of urea to their immediate donors, and calculate ATP molecules and high-energy phosphate equivalents consumed.
2. Relate transamination, glutamate, aspartate, urea-cycle compartmentation, CPS1 regulation and fumarate return to central metabolism.
3. Convert a stated nitrogen-disposal load into a stoichiometric urea amount and the associated ATP-equivalent demand under an explicit closed-balance assumption.

## Collect amino nitrogen and deliver it safely

Many aminotransferases transfer an amino group to alpha-ketoglutarate, producing glutamate and the corresponding alpha-keto acid. Glutamate can release ammonium through oxidative deamination, or transfer nitrogen to oxaloacetate to form aspartate. In a hepatic bookkeeping model, ammonium supplies one urea nitrogen and aspartate supplies the other. The carbon skeleton of the original amino acid follows a separate route into intermediates such as pyruvate, acetyl-CoA or citric-acid-cycle compounds.

## Follow the atoms through mitochondrial and cytosolic reactions

The first two reactions occur in the mitochondrial matrix. Carbamoyl-phosphate synthetase I (CPS1) combines ammonium and bicarbonate, consuming 2 ATP to make carbamoyl phosphate. N-acetylglutamate is an essential allosteric activator of CPS1. Ornithine transcarbamylase transfers the carbamoyl group to ornithine, forming citrulline, which is transported to the cytosol.

In the cytosol, argininosuccinate synthetase joins citrulline and aspartate. This step uses ATP and forms AMP plus pyrophosphate, consuming two high-energy phosphate equivalents. Argininosuccinate lyase then produces arginine and fumarate. Arginase hydrolyzes arginine to urea and ornithine; ornithine returns to the mitochondrion to continue the cycle.

The urea carbon comes from bicarbonate. Its two nitrogens come from ammonium (through carbamoyl phosphate) and aspartate. Per urea, the cycle uses **3 ATP molecules but 4 high-energy phosphate equivalents**: two ATP are hydrolyzed to ADP at CPS1, and one ATP is cleaved to AMP at argininosuccinate synthesis. Counting only the number of ATP molecules hides the two-bond cost of the ATP-to-AMP reaction.

## Connect nitrogen disposal to the citric acid cycle

Fumarate produced by argininosuccinate lyase can be hydrated to malate and oxidized to oxaloacetate. Transamination of oxaloacetate forms aspartate, which can return nitrogen to argininosuccinate synthesis. This aspartate–argininosuccinate connection links the urea cycle with central carbon metabolism, but it does not mean that the urea cycle is simply a branch of the citric acid cycle. The carbon and nitrogen balances should be followed separately.

## Worked example

**Problem.** A synthetic liver-cell model has 5 mol of nitrogen atoms available for urea formation. Assuming complete disposal through this cycle, with the two nitrogen donors paired as described here, how many mol urea, ATP molecules and high-energy phosphate equivalents are required?

**Step 1: pair nitrogen atoms.** Each urea contains two nitrogen atoms, so 5 mol nitrogen atoms can form 2.5 mol urea under this idealized balance.

**Step 2: scale the energy ledger.** Per mol urea, the cycle uses 3 mol ATP molecules and 4 mol high-energy phosphate equivalents. Therefore, 2.5 mol urea uses **7.5 mol ATP molecules** and **10 mol high-energy phosphate equivalents**.

The example ignores transport, nitrogen recovery in other products and changes in intermediate pools. Its purpose is to distinguish moles of ATP from the number of high-energy phosphate bonds spent. It gives a stoichiometric demand under the stated closed-balance assumption, not a pathway rate or a prediction of the cell's net ATP pool change; actual output can be limited by substrate delivery, enzyme activity and other nitrogen fates.

## Common mistakes

- Assigning both urea nitrogens to free ammonia; one comes from aspartate.
- Assigning the urea carbon to an amino acid instead of bicarbonate.
- Saying the cycle consumes four ATP molecules; it consumes three ATP molecules equal to four high-energy phosphate equivalents.
- Treating a nitrogen load as an observed urea-production rate rather than a closed stoichiometric input.
- Putting all five reactions in one compartment; CPS1 and ornithine transcarbamylase are mitochondrial, while the later reactions are cytosolic.
- Omitting fumarate release or treating fumarate as a nitrogen donor.
- Treating amino-acid nitrogen transfer as if the carbon skeleton must enter the same pathway.
