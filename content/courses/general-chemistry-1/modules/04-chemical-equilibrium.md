# Chemical equilibrium

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:

1. Interpret the reaction quotient Q and equilibrium constant K for a reaction at a stated temperature.
2. Predict the direction of net change by comparing Q with K.

## Equilibrium is dynamic

A reversible reaction can proceed in both directions. In a closed system at equilibrium, the forward and reverse reaction rates are equal. Molecules continue reacting in both directions, but the macroscopic concentrations remain constant because neither direction has a net rate advantage. Equilibrium is not a stopped reaction, and constant concentrations do not mean reactants and products are present in equal amounts.

The equilibrium composition depends on the reaction and conditions. Some systems contain mostly products at equilibrium; others contain mostly reactants. The equilibrium constant K describes the composition relationship at equilibrium for a particular balanced reaction and temperature. Its magnitude does not tell how quickly equilibrium is reached. A very large K can describe a reaction that approaches equilibrium slowly; rate requires kinetic information.

## Build the reaction quotient from the equation

For a balanced reaction such as aA + bB ⇌ cC + dD, the concentration-based reaction quotient has the product concentrations in the numerator and reactant concentrations in the denominator, each raised to its stoichiometric coefficient:

Q_c = [C]^c [D]^d / ([A]^a [B]^b)

The same mass-action expression can be evaluated for a mixture before it reaches equilibrium. At equilibrium, its value is K_c. Thus Q describes the mixture’s present composition, while K is the equilibrium target at the stated temperature. They use the same expression for a given reaction, but they need not have the same numerical value at an arbitrary moment.

For N₂O₄(g) ⇌ 2 NO₂(g), Q_c = [NO₂]²/[N₂O₄]. The coefficient 2 becomes an exponent; it does not become a multiplier outside the concentration. For a heterogeneous equilibrium, a pure solid or liquid is not included as a concentration term in the introductory expression because its activity is treated as constant. Aqueous species and gases are represented using the corresponding concentration- or pressure-based form. This lesson uses the standard introductory concentration model and does not derive activity corrections for nonideal solutions.

If a reaction is reversed, its equilibrium constant is inverted. If all coefficients are multiplied by a factor, the corresponding equilibrium expression is raised to that factor and K changes accordingly. Always begin with the exact balanced equation and phase labels rather than memorizing a formula without its reaction.

## Compare Q with K

At a fixed temperature, the comparison indicates the direction of net change:

| Comparison | Present mixture | Predicted net change |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Q < K | The product-to-reactant ratio is below its equilibrium value. | Forward, toward products, until Q reaches K. |
| Q > K | The product-to-reactant ratio is above its equilibrium value. | Reverse, toward reactants, until Q reaches K. |
| Q = K | The mixture has the equilibrium composition relationship. | No net shift; forward and reverse reactions continue at equal rates. |

For the first practice question, Q = 0.2 and K = 5. Since 0.2 < 5, the forward direction forms products. In the second question, Q = 0.5 and K = 2; again Q < K, so the net change proceeds toward products. The two prompts use different numbers but the same comparison. Neither requires the reaction enthalpy to decide direction when Q and K at the same temperature are supplied.

As a separate constructed example, consider A ⇌ 2B with concentrations [A] = 1.0 M and [B] = 0.50 M. Its quotient is Q = [B]²/[A] = 0.25 under the simplified numerical convention. If K for that reaction at the same temperature is 1, then Q < K and the forward direction is favored. This made-up value illustrates the decision rule, not a measurement. Thermodynamically rigorous equilibrium constants use activities and are dimensionless; introductory concentration expressions treat the standard-state normalization in a simplified way.

## Disturbances and the return to equilibrium

Changing a reactant or product concentration changes Q immediately because the composition has changed. At the same temperature, K remains fixed, so the new Q/K comparison predicts which net direction moves the system toward equilibrium. For example, adding a reactant to an equilibrium mixture generally makes the product-to-reactant quotient smaller than K; net forward reaction then consumes some of the added reactant. Removing a product can produce the same directional result. The size of the shift depends on the system’s reaction and amounts, not on the slogan alone.

Temperature is different: changing temperature can change K because the relative energetics of the forward and reverse processes change. A catalyst lowers kinetic barriers for both directions and can help equilibrium be reached sooner, but it does not change K or the equilibrium composition at fixed temperature. A catalyst changes the route or rate, not the thermodynamic endpoint.

Pressure or volume changes matter for gas mixtures when they change the relevant partial pressures and the reaction has different total gas coefficients on each side. A general “shift to fewer moles” shortcut can fail if phases or the actual quotient are overlooked. The safer procedure is to identify the new conditions, determine whether K changed, calculate or compare the new Q, and then infer the net direction.

## A reliable reasoning sequence

1. Write the balanced reversible equation and retain phase labels.
2. Construct Q with products over reactants and use coefficients as exponents; omit pure solids and liquids in the introductory expression.
3. Evaluate Q from the current composition and obtain K for the same reaction and temperature.
4. Compare Q with K: below means net forward, above means net reverse, equal means no net change.
5. Separate composition from kinetics: Q/K predicts direction, not the time required to reach equilibrium.

## Common errors and limits

- Do not equate equilibrium with equal reactant and product concentrations; it means equal forward and reverse rates.
- Do not treat equilibrium as a static state in which molecular reactions stop.
- Do not confuse Q, which varies with the current mixture, with K, which is fixed for a given reaction at a given temperature.
- Do not reverse the Q/K direction: Q < K requires net product formation; Q > K requires net reactant formation.
- Do not assume a catalyst changes K or the final equilibrium composition.
- Do not infer a rate or a reaction enthalpy from the Q/K comparison alone.
- The concentration expressions here use introductory idealized conventions and do not replace activity-based thermodynamics for nonideal mixtures.
