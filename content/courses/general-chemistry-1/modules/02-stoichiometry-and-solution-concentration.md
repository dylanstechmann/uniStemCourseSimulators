# Stoichiometry and solution concentration

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:

1. Use a balanced chemical equation as a mole-ratio map before converting amounts.
2. Identify a limiting reagent, calculate theoretical product, and use molarity and dilution relationships with units.

## Equations are quantitative maps

A chemical equation represents a proposed transformation. Before using it for a calculation, balance it so that each element and the net charge are conserved. The coefficients then give relative amounts in moles. They do not directly compare grams, liters of gas at unspecified conditions, or solution volumes. Convert a measured quantity to moles first, apply the coefficient ratio, and then convert to the requested unit.

For the balanced equation 2 H₂ + O₂ → 2 H₂O, the coefficient ratio says that 2 mol H₂ react with 1 mol O₂ to make 2 mol H₂O. A useful conversion factor may be written with product moles over reactant moles, such as 2 mol H₂O / 2 mol H₂. The units cancel and the coefficient labels tell you which direction the factor converts. Reversing it is valid only if the numerator and denominator are also reversed. A mole ratio is not a mass ratio: hydrogen and oxygen have different molar masses.

## Find the limiting reagent

When more than one reactant amount is supplied, convert each amount to moles. For each reactant, divide its mole amount by its coefficient in the balanced equation. The smallest value is the maximum reaction extent allowed by that reactant. That reactant is limiting; the others are in excess. You can also calculate the possible product independently from each reactant and select the smaller product amount. Both methods are equivalent when the equation and units are correct.

Divide by their coefficients: H₂ permits about 0.992 mol of reaction extent, whereas O₂ permits 1.000 mol. Hydrogen is therefore limiting in this constructed calculation. The equation forms two moles of water per mole of extent, so the theoretical amount is about 1.984 mol H₂O, or about 35.7 g using 18.015 g/mol. At two significant figures, that mass would be reported as 36 g. A theoretical yield is a conservation-based maximum under the stated model; it is not a prediction that an actual process recovers that mass.

The “divide by coefficient” step is a compact comparison. It avoids guessing which mass looks smaller or simply choosing the reactant with fewer moles. If a reactant is absent, has the wrong physical state for the stipulated model, or participates in a different reaction, the supplied information may be insufficient. A limiting-reagent result is conditional on the balanced equation representing the reaction that actually occurs.

## Concentration and solution volume

Molarity, written M, is amount of solute divided by total solution volume in liters:

M = n(solute) / V(solution)

The denominator is the final volume of the solution, not the volume of solvent before dissolving. Rearranging gives n = M × V when volume is in liters. Dimensional analysis makes the units visible: mol/L multiplied by L gives mol. For example, 0.10 mol/L × 0.300 L = 0.030 mol. Convert milliliters to liters before using the molarity definition unless both sides of a ratio use the same volume unit.

A dilution lowers concentration by adding solvent while the amount of dissolved solute stays fixed. Equating the solute amount before and after gives C₁V₁ = C₂V₂. This relation assumes no reaction, precipitation, transfer loss, or change in which solute is counted; it uses the final solution volume. For 300 mL of a 0.10 M target made from 1.5 M stock, V₁ = (0.10 M × 300 mL)/(1.5 M) = 20 mL. The 20 mL is the stock portion; the preparation is brought to a final volume of 300 mL. It is not generally sound to assume that adding exactly 280 mL solvent produces exactly 300 mL solution, because volumes need not be additive.

The same concentration relation supports reaction calculations. If a known volume and molarity of a solution react, first compute dissolved moles. Then use the balanced equation to convert those moles to another reactant or product amount. Keep concentration conversion and chemical stoichiometry as separate steps so the units reveal which operation is happening.

## A reliable calculation sequence

1. Write or verify the balanced equation, including physical states or charge when they affect the model.
2. Convert each supplied mass, solution volume, or particle count to moles using an appropriate conversion.
3. Use coefficient ratios in the direction needed; cancel units at each step.
4. For multiple reactants, compare amount divided by coefficient or compare candidate product amounts.
5. Convert the final amount to mass, volume, or concentration only after the mole relationship is complete.
6. Check that the answer has the requested dimensions and a sensible magnitude; round only at the reporting step.

This sequence also helps diagnose errors. A mass-to-mass coefficient ratio usually signals that molar mass was skipped. A concentration calculation in which milliliters are treated as liters is off by a factor of one thousand. A dilution answer larger than the final volume or a stock volume inconsistent with target concentration deserves a second check. The units and conservation statements are part of the model, not decorative annotations.

## Common errors and limits

- Do not balance an equation by changing subscripts in chemical formulas; coefficients change the number of formula units.
- Do not use coefficients as ratios of grams. Convert mass to moles first.
- Do not identify the limiting reagent by selecting the smallest raw mass or mole count; compare stoichiometric amounts.
- Do not confuse theoretical yield with actual recovered product.
- Do not substitute solvent volume for final solution volume in a molarity calculation.
- Do not apply C₁V₁ = C₂V₂ across a reaction or loss of solute without adding the missing material balance.
- Significant-figure and uncertainty rules depend on stated measurements and reporting policy; preserve guard digits during calculation and report to the precision justified by the inputs.

The examples are calculations from stated values, not laboratory instructions or a chemical-handling recommendation. Real reactions can have incomplete conversion, side reactions, measurement uncertainty and recovery losses that are absent from the ideal material-balance model.
