# Bonding, geometry and molecular properties

## Learning objectives

1. Count valence electrons and formal charges in selected Lewis structures.
2. Distinguish electron-domain geometry from molecular shape and bond-vector polarity.
3. Relate intermolecular interactions to properties while stating model limitations.

## Electron accounting before a drawing

A Lewis structure tracks valence electrons and connectivity. Add the main-group valence contributions, add electrons for negative charge and subtract them for positive charge. CO₂ has 4+2×6=16 valence electrons. NH₄⁺ has 5+4×1−1=8. An ion's charge is part of its electron budget; using the neutral-atom total without the charge correction changes the problem.

In the conventional ammonium Lewis structure, nitrogen has four single bonds and no lone pair. Formal charge is valence count minus nonbonding electrons minus half the bonding electrons. Nitrogen therefore has 5−0−8/2=+1, and each hydrogen has zero. Formal charges sum to the net ion charge. Formal charge is a bookkeeping assignment, not a claim that the entire physical charge density sits at one atom.

For water, oxygen supplies six valence electrons and the hydrogens supply two more. Two O–H bonds use four electrons, leaving four as two oxygen lone pairs. For carbon dioxide, the conventional two-double-bond structure satisfies the octets with two lone pairs on each oxygen. A multiple bond counts as one direction or electron domain in the introductory geometry model, although its electron density and bond strength differ from a single bond.

## Geometry and shape

VSEPR describes arrangements that reduce repulsion among valence electron domains. A double or triple bond counts as one domain. Three domains have trigonal planar electron-domain geometry with ideal angles of 120°. In the introductory BF₃ model all three domains are bonds, so the molecule itself is trigonal planar. When one of three domains is a lone pair, as in the introductory SO₂ model, the molecular shape is bent and the angle is expected to be somewhat smaller than 120°. Four domains have tetrahedral electron-domain geometry. If all four are bonding directions, the ideal angle is arccos(−1/3), about 109.471°. Water has four domains but only two bonded atoms, so its molecular shape is bent. Ammonia has three bonds and one lone pair, giving a trigonal pyramidal molecular shape. Electron-domain geometry includes lone pairs; molecular shape describes atom positions.

These labels are models, not numerical guarantees that all bond angles equal ideal values. Lone pairs, different substituents and multiple bonding can alter angles. A structure with two bonding domains and no central lone pair, such as the introductory CO₂ model, is linear. Its symmetric geometry matters when combining bond dipoles.

A geometric drawing must also distinguish a two-dimensional representation from the three-dimensional molecule. Four lines on a page do not make a tetrahedral molecule square planar. More advanced bonding and coordination examples can require models beyond the simple main-group VSEPR cases used here. This lesson does not assign every metal complex a shape from an elementary domain count.

## Bond polarity versus molecular polarity

A bond dipole is a vector. Molecular polarity depends on their combination and the full electron distribution. Two equal collinear bond vectors pointing in opposite directions cancel, as in the symmetric two-bond model of CO₂. Polar bonds do not therefore imply a polar molecule. Conversely, a bent arrangement generally prevents that simple cancellation.

For a synthetic two-bond model, assign each bond a dipole magnitude of 1 arbitrary dipole unit and a separation angle of 104.5°. Their resultant magnitude is 2cos(104.5°/2), approximately 1.224435 units. This is vector geometry with deliberately assigned dipoles, not the measured dipole moment of water. If the separation were 180°, the same equal vectors would sum to zero. Different bond magnitudes would not completely cancel merely because they were opposite.

Resonance structures describe alternative electron-bookkeeping forms for one delocalized species. The molecule is not assumed to switch repeatedly between drawings as a classical process. Equivalent resonance contributors can imply equal average bond character; one drawing alone may misrepresent the actual electron distribution. Counting valid contributors is not the same as counting distinct molecular species in a sample.

## Molecular orbitals, bond order and magnetism

Lewis structures count localized electron pairs and predict many main-group shapes. They do not explain every bond-strength or magnetic result. In the introductory molecular-orbital (MO) model, atomic orbitals combine into orbitals spread over both nuclei. Bonding MOs lower energy and add electron density between the nuclei; antibonding MOs, marked with an asterisk, oppose bonding. Once electrons are filled in the stated order, calculate

$$\text{bond order}=\frac{N_{bonding}-N_{antibonding}}{2}.$$

A positive bond order supports a net bond in this simple model. Among closely related species, removing an electron from an antibonding orbital raises bond order; this is a qualitative comparison, not a formula for exact bond energy or length.

For second-row homonuclear diatomics, the supplied relative order matters. In the N₂ model, the two π(2p) orbitals lie below σ(2p), reflecting s–p mixing. N₂ has 10 valence electrons; filling σ(2s)², σ*(2s)², π(2p)⁴, σ(2p)² gives eight bonding and two antibonding electrons, so bond order is 3. All occupied orbitals are paired, making this model diamagnetic.

For O₂, use σ(2p) below π(2p). Its 12 valence electrons fill σ(2s)², σ*(2s)², σ(2p)², π(2p)⁴, then place one electron in each of the two equal-energy π*(2p) orbitals before pairing. There are eight bonding and four antibonding electrons, so bond order is 2; the two unpaired electrons make the molecule paramagnetic. Removing one electron to form O₂⁺ removes it from a π* orbital and raises the model bond order to 2.5.

This comparison illustrates why a Lewis single/double/triple-bond drawing cannot by itself account for O₂'s unpaired electrons. The orbital order and occupancy are assumptions of a qualitative diatomic model. It omits calculated orbital energies, electron correlation details and quantitative dissociation energies. ## Interactions and properties

All molecules exhibit dispersion interactions. Polar molecules can also have permanent-dipole interactions. Introductory hydrogen bonding involves an appropriate hydrogen attached to an electronegative atom and a compatible acceptor; simply containing an oxygen or nitrogen is not enough to establish every donor role. In ammonium, nitrogen's conventional Lewis structure has no available lone pair, so its role differs from ammonia's even though both contain nitrogen and hydrogen.

“Like dissolves like” is a qualitative starting point, not a quantitative solubility theorem. Dissolution depends on the energy and entropy changes associated with separating old interactions and forming new ones. Ionic compounds are not guaranteed to be highly soluble merely because water is polar. ## Worked example

For NH₄⁺, start with 8 valence electrons, assign them to four N–H bonds and calculate nitrogen formal charge +1. Four bonding domains give tetrahedral geometry. For O₂, 12 valence electrons fill eight bonding and four antibonding MOs, giving bond order 2 and two unpaired π* electrons. For O₂⁺, removing one antibonding electron raises bond order to 2.5. Lewis electron accounting, molecular shape, dipole-vector sums and delocalized MO occupancy answer related but distinct questions.

## Common mistakes and checks

Do not forget the ionic charge in the electron budget. Do not equate formal charge with a complete measured charge distribution. Do not count a double bond as two independent VSEPR directions. Distinguish molecular shape from domain geometry, and check vector cancellation before inferring polarity. A qualitative interaction label alone does not provide a numerical boiling point or solubility.

## Limits of this lesson

The dipole magnitudes are synthetic, and the molecular examples illustrate standard introductory models. The lesson does not perform a quantum electronic-structure calculation or predict actual property values for an unknown material. No experimental procedure is supplied.
