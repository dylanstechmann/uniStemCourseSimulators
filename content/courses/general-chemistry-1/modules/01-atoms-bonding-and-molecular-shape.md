# Electron configuration and periodic trends

An electron configuration records how an atom's electrons occupy shells and subshells in a stated ground-state model. It connects the atomic number to valence-electron patterns and helps explain broad periodic trends. This short foundation focuses on that link. The later bonding lesson develops Lewis structures, VSEPR, molecular polarity and molecular-orbital bond reasoning.

## Write a configuration and count valence electrons

For a neutral atom, the number of electrons equals its atomic number. In the introductory ground-state filling model, oxygen has configuration 1s² 2s² 2p⁴ and six electrons in its outer shell; sodium is [Ne]3s¹ and chlorine is [Ne]3s²3p⁵. A configuration is an orbital-occupancy model, not a set of classical electron paths. For ions, add or remove electrons according to charge while keeping proton number fixed unless a nuclear change is specified.

The configurations help distinguish the main-group valence pattern from the total electron count. Atoms in a column often share an outer-shell pattern, though transition elements, ions and exceptions require attention to the actual species. Do not infer a complete bonding structure from the configuration alone.

## Explain broad periodic trends

Across a period, proton number rises while the added electrons enter the same principal shell. Shielding does not increase by the same amount, so effective nuclear charge generally grows and pulls the outer electrons closer. Atomic radius therefore tends to decrease across a period. Down a group, an additional occupied shell places the valence electrons farther from the nucleus and increases shielding, so atomic radius tends to increase.

The same attraction-and-distance reasoning helps compare first ionization energies: removing an electron is generally harder when the outer electron is more strongly attracted to the nucleus. But ionization energies are not a perfectly monotonic staircase. Subshell energies and electron pairing can create local exceptions; compare the specific configurations when adjacent elements do not follow the broad trend. Electron affinity and electronegativity are related to electron attraction but are distinct measured or defined properties, so do not treat their trend arrows as interchangeable.

**Worked comparison.** Na ([Ne]3s¹) and Cl ([Ne]3s²3p⁵) are in the same period. The larger proton number in Cl acts on electrons in the same outer principal shell, increasing effective nuclear charge in the broad model; Cl is therefore smaller than Na. Comparing Na with K down Group 1, K's valence electron occupies a higher shell and is farther from the nucleus, so K is larger. These are qualitative predictions, not exact radii or rules without exceptions.

## Checks and limits

Write the species and its charge before counting electrons. Keep a ground-state configuration distinct from a bonding model. Use shielding and shell distance to justify a trend, then check for subshell or pairing exceptions rather than relying on a memorized arrow. These introductory rules do not calculate wavefunctions, exact ionization energies or full periodic-property tables.
