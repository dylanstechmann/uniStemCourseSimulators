# Week 8 synthesis: quantitative models and their assumptions

This reading gathers quantitative reasoning from weeks 1–7 into one reusable sequence. It introduces no new dataset or graded work. Use it alongside the public cumulative practice set; the practice remains ungraded and is not a midterm.

## Start with the question and the quantity

Before calculating, write the biological question in one sentence and identify the quantity that could answer it. A pH comparison concerns protonation under a stated environment. An equilibrium potential concerns the balance of chemical and electrical contributions for an ion. An initial enzyme rate concerns product formation over an early time window. A microscopy estimate concerns the spatial scale that can be resolved or sampled. These quantities answer different questions; a number alone does not choose among them.

Record the inputs, units, and conditions before substituting values. Check that a reported concentration, length, time, or rate is on the scale assumed by the model. Preserve the distinction between a measured quantity and a derived one, and write down whether the calculation describes a single observation, a mean, or a model prediction.

## Make assumptions visible

Every compact model simplifies biology. The Henderson–Hasselbalch relation connects pH and pKa under the stated acid–base model; it does not report the local environment unless that environment was measured. The Nernst relation predicts the equilibrium potential for a specified ion and concentration ratio; it does not tell whether a membrane pathway is open or how quickly the ion crosses. A transporter can be passive or energy-coupled, so identify the route before interpreting movement.

The initial-rate Michaelis–Menten model depends on conditions such as a defined substrate, measured initial rates, and an appropriate steady-state approximation. Km is a model parameter, not automatically a binding constant. An enzyme changes the rate at which a system approaches equilibrium; it does not by itself change the reaction’s overall free-energy difference or equilibrium position. For coupled reactions, state which reaction supplies the favorable free-energy change and whether the coupling is physically specified.

A microscope has separate limits for resolution, sampling, and contrast. Pixel size alone cannot establish that two signals are biologically co-localized, just as a model output alone cannot establish mechanism. In each case, distinguish the quantity calculated from the biological interpretation attached to it.

## Test sensitivity and limits

Ask which assumption, if changed, could reverse or weaken the conclusion. Check units and signs, then vary one uncertain input at a time. If a conclusion depends on an unmeasured local pH, an assumed transport route, a chosen initial-rate window, or a pixel-size estimate near the sampling limit, say so. Report the result at a precision supported by the inputs. Do not turn an illustrative model prediction into an observation.

## A reusable scratch-pad

For any cumulative-practice item, try four lines before reading the feedback:

1. **Target:** What quantity or comparison is requested?
2. **Model:** Which relationship connects the supplied evidence to that target?
3. **Conditions:** Which assumptions, units, and controls make that relationship usable?
4. **Boundary:** What does the result support, and what additional evidence would be needed for the next claim?

A correct calculation can still support a narrow conclusion. The goal of this review is to carry the assumptions with the number, not to treat a formula as a substitute for biological evidence.
