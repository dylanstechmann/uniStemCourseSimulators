# Assignment 1: distinguish an aryl mechanism with an explicit atom map

**Open, ungraded paper practice with substantial AI-assisted authoring.** Proposed placement: block 10 after aromatic substitution and spectroscopic constraints. All branching, conversion and observation values are invented. ## Part A: define the ring before naming a product

Use a six-carbon cycle C1–C2–C3–C4–C5–C6–C1. The leaving group is on C1, an inert substituent replaces the hydrogen at C6, and a paper tag identifies C4. The declared formation rule removes the leaving group and one adjacent hydrogen to produce one aryne edge; no other precursor mechanism is allowed in this exercise.

Identify which neighboring site can supply that hydrogen. Draw or describe the allowed intermediate without renumbering the original carbon atoms. Then map nitrogen-nucleophile addition at each end of the intermediate, followed by protonation of the other end. State the position of the new C–N bond and the tag's relationship to it for each path.

Classify ortho, meta and para by shortest ring-edge distance one, two and three. A name based on a newly numbered product can obscure original atom identity; provide the label map even if you also give a conventional positional description. The carbon tag remains on C4 throughout the stipulated process.

## Part B: keep branching and conversion denominators separate

Stipulate complete selectivity between the two allowed addition paths after aryne formation: 70% of consumed material takes the C1-attachment path and 30% the other path. Overall starting-material conversion is 75%, with no additional products. Begin with 2.00 mmol of the paper substrate.

Calculate the fractions and amounts of each product relative to the original inventory, plus the unreacted starting amount. Check both total amount and the conditional product ratio. Explain why “70% branch fraction” does not mean 70% isolated target yield from the starting inventory when conversion is incomplete.

Do not infer the 70:30 value from the existence of two arrows. It is supplied separately. The exercise includes no kinetic constants, isolation efficiencies, detector response factors or real selectivity measurements. If you introduce any of these in an extension, label them as additional assumptions and keep their denominators visible.

## Part C: contrast alternative mechanisms

Compare two mechanism hypotheses. H1 attaches the nucleophile only at the carbon that originally held the leaving group; H2 uses the stipulated aryne and permits attachment at either endpoint. For each, list which original C–N bond placements are allowed. Identify an observation that could discriminate them and an observation, such as an elemental formula alone, that could not.

Describe at least two possible limitations of a real tagging study: incomplete observation of minor products, parallel pathways, tag effects, response calibration, or uncertain positional assignment. Consistency with H2 is not a universal proof of H2's uniqueness. A rigorous argument names the alternatives actually tested.

Now replace both neighboring hydrogens at C2 and C6 by inert blocking substituents. Under the declared leaving-group/ortho-hydrogen formation rule, explain why the aryne path is unavailable. Do not extend that conclusion to every possible chemistry: an activated addition–elimination pathway or a different aryne precursor would require a separate hypothesis and its own conditions.

## Part D: a separate formula check

For an unsubstituted formula exercise, C6H5X formally loses HX to give C6H4. Addition of NH2− followed by H+ gives C6H7N. Verify carbon, hydrogen and nitrogen counts and calculate DBE before and after addition. This is a separate unsubstituted case; do not apply its formula unchanged to the blocked-ring model, whose extra substituent has not been assigned an elemental formula.

Explain why atom counts and DBE are necessary consistency checks but insufficient to establish mechanism, constitutional identity or a product ratio. Keep formal charge through the nucleophilic addition and carbon protonation rather than adding all hydrogens directly to nitrogen.
