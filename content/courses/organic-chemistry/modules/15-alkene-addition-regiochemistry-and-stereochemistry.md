# Alkene addition: regioselectivity and stereochemistry

Elimination in the previous block makes an alkene by removing groups from adjacent carbons. Addition uses the alkene's π bond to form two new σ bonds. The outcome depends on the mechanism: a carbocation route can favor one regioisomer and may rearrange, a cyclic halonium intermediate leads to anti addition, and hydroboration proceeds with syn, non-Markovnikov orientation. These are original paper models, not procedures for carrying out reactions.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:

1. Predict the usual regiochemistry of hydrohalogenation and hydration of an unsymmetrical alkene, with the mechanistic assumption stated.
2. Distinguish carbocation, halonium-ion and hydroboration pathways by their stereochemical outcomes and rearrangement limits.
3. Check addition products with carbon, hydrogen, halogen and degree-of-unsaturation bookkeeping.

## Hydrohalogenation: regiochemistry through a carbocation

For addition of HX to an unsymmetrical alkene in the simple ionic model, the π bond accepts a proton and a carbon–halogen bond forms in a later step. Protonation is favored in the direction that produces the more stable carbocation; halide then captures that intermediate. In the usual Markovnikov description, hydrogen bonds to the less substituted alkene carbon and X to the more substituted carbon. This is a regiochemical prediction, not a stereochemical rule.

For example, addition of HBr to 2-methylpropene is represented by formation of a tertiary carbocation followed by bromide capture, giving 2-bromo-2-methylpropane as the predicted constitutional product. The carbocation model permits rotation and can allow rearrangement when a more stable cation is accessible. It does not promise one exclusive product for every substrate or condition.

## Halogenation: anti addition through a bridged intermediate

Addition of Br₂ to a simple alkene is modeled through a three-membered bromonium ion rather than a free, planar carbocation. Bromide opens the bridged intermediate from the opposite face. The two bromine atoms therefore add anti in the ideal model. With cyclohexene, this predicts the trans relationship in 1,2-dibromocyclohexane. A free-carbocation rearrangement is not the proposed pathway here; do not transfer the HX mechanism to every alkene addition.

The bridged intermediate constrains which face the second group can approach. In an acyclic substrate, multiple conformations and faces can still produce distinct stereoisomers. State the relative stereochemistry supported by the mechanism; assign R/S only after drawing the actual product and ranking its groups.

## Hydration: two complementary routes

Acid-catalyzed hydration follows the protonation/carbocation pattern: water captures the cation, and deprotonation gives an alcohol. Its usual orientation is Markovnikov. A carbocation can rearrange, and a planar intermediate can be attacked from either face; a product's exact stereoisomer ratio does not follow from regiochemistry alone.

Hydroboration–oxidation gives a complementary result. In the addition step, B and H attach to the same face of the double bond, with boron usually at the less substituted carbon. Oxidation replaces the C–B bond by C–OH with retention at that carbon. The overall hydration is therefore syn and non-Markovnikov in the standard introductory model, with no carbocation rearrangement in the hydroboration step.

For 1-butene, acid hydration predicts mainly 2-butanol, while hydroboration–oxidation predicts 1-butanol. The outcomes differ because the pathways place the new C–O bond at different carbons. Both products have formula C₄H₁₀O; formula alone cannot identify which alcohol formed. The example illustrates connectivity and mechanism, not a measured yield.

## Worked example: check an addition claim

For 1-butene, C₄H₈, the degree of unsaturation is $(2(4)+2-8)/2=1$, accounted for by the C=C bond. In an HBr addition, the C=C becomes C–C and H plus Br are added, giving C₄H₉Br with zero degrees of unsaturation: $(2(4)+2-9-1)/2=0$. A proposed product that retains the alkene while also adding H and Br has an inconsistent bond/atom inventory for this one-step addition model.

## Common errors and limits

- Treating Markovnikov as a synonym for syn or anti; it describes which carbon receives which group.
- Using a free carbocation to explain halogenation when the standard model uses a bridged halonium ion.
- Assuming all additions avoid rearrangement or give only one stereoisomer.
- Predicting absolute configuration from a reagent label without drawing the product and assigning priorities.
- Treating synthetic product predictions as yield, purity, experimental observation or synthesis guidance.
