# Substitution mechanisms: SN1 and SN2

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:

1. Use substrate structure, nucleophile, solvent, and leaving-group ability to compare SN1 and SN2 pathways without treating a single feature as decisive.
2. Relate each mechanism to its rate law and to the stereochemical outcome at a reacting stereocenter.

## What substitution means

In a nucleophilic substitution, an electron-pair donor replaces a leaving group bonded to an electrophilic carbon. A curved arrow begins at the electron pair or bond that supplies electrons and points to the atom or bond that receives them. The product drawing must account for the new carbon–nucleophile bond, the departing group, formal charges, and any proton transfers. The same overall change can occur through different sequences of bond making and bond breaking; the mechanism determines what a rate measurement or stereochemical observation can support.

SN2 is a one-step, concerted pathway. The nucleophile approaches the carbon from the side opposite the leaving group while the carbon–nucleophile bond forms and the carbon–leaving-group bond breaks. There is one transition state, not a discrete carbocation intermediate. For the simple bimolecular model, the rate law is `rate = k[substrate][nucleophile]`: changing either reactant concentration can change the initial rate.

SN1 is a stepwise pathway. In its simple limiting model, the substrate first ionizes as the leaving group departs, producing a carbocation; nucleophile capture follows. If ionization is rate-limiting, the rate law is `rate = k[substrate]`. The nucleophile does not appear in that rate law because it is not involved in the slow step. A real mechanism can include ion pairs, solvent cages, reversibility, or other steps, so a measured rate law is evidence about a system under specified conditions rather than a universal label.

## Compare the evidence together

| Feature | SN2 tendency | SN1 tendency |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Carbon environment | Less crowded methyl and primary centers usually permit backside approach; secondary centers are condition-sensitive. | A substrate that can form a more stable carbocation is favored; ordinary tertiary centers are common examples. |
| Nucleophile | A reactive, available nucleophile often favors the concerted pathway. | Nucleophile strength and concentration are not part of the simple rate-limiting ionization step, though they can affect later capture and product distribution. |
| Solvent | Polar aprotic solvents often leave an anionic nucleophile relatively available for attack. | Polar, protic media can stabilize separated ions and favor ionization. |
| Leaving group | Departure occurs as the new bond forms; a group able to leave more readily can assist. | Departure creates the carbocation, so leaving-group ability and carbocation stability both matter. |
| Stereochemistry | Backside attack gives geometric inversion at the reacting tetrahedral center. | A planar carbocation can be attacked from either face; products may show partial racemization. |

These are interacting tendencies, not a checklist that mechanically determines the answer. Methyl and primary substrates generally disfavor ordinary carbocation formation; tertiary centers strongly obstruct SN2 backside approach. Secondary substrates often require the most careful comparison. Allylic or benzylic resonance stabilization can alter the usual substrate trend. A strong base, heat, or an accessible beta hydrogen can also make elimination compete. State the substrate and conditions before making a prediction, and do not infer a mechanism from solvent or nucleophile alone.

The rate laws give a compact way to distinguish the simplified kinetic models. Consider a deliberately synthetic initial-rate comparison with substrate concentration held fixed while nucleophile concentration changes from 0.10 to 0.20 mol/L. The SN2 model predicts a twofold initial-rate increase because the nucleophile concentration doubles. The simple SN1 model predicts no direct change in its rate-limiting ionization rate. This is an illustration of the equations, not an experimental measurement or a claim that all observed systems follow ideal rate laws. If substrate concentration doubled as well, the SN2 model would predict a fourfold rate; the SN1 model would predict a twofold rate.

## Stereochemistry: describe geometry before labels

At a stereogenic carbon, SN2 backside attack changes the three-dimensional arrangement by inversion. Do not automatically translate “inversion” into an `R`-to-`S` label change: replacing one substituent can change the priority ranking used for those descriptors. First compare the spatial arrangement, then assign priorities in each structure independently.

An SN1 carbocation is approximately trigonal planar at the cationic center, so a nucleophile can approach either face. If those faces are equally accessible and the intermediate loses its original stereochemical information, a racemic mixture is a useful idealized limit. In many reactions, the departing ion remains nearby or the solvent cage is not fully escaped; the two faces may not be equally available. “SN1” therefore supports loss of a single enforced backside trajectory, but it does not guarantee an exactly 50:50 mixture in every case. A neighboring stereocenter or an already chiral environment can also make the faces nonequivalent.

## Work through a mechanism choice

For a tertiary alkyl halide reacting in water, the carbon is strongly hindered for the backside approach required by SN2. Water is a polar protic solvent that can stabilize ions, and a tertiary carbocation is comparatively stabilized. SN1 is therefore the more plausible substitution pathway in the simplified comparison. The prediction remains conditional: substrate details, competing elimination, and actual rate or product evidence can refine it.

For a chiral secondary alkyl bromide undergoing concerted attack by cyanide, the stated one-step backside mechanism determines the qualitative outcome even before all conditions are known. The reacting center inverts geometrically, and the simple rate model is `rate = k[RBr][CN⁻]`. Both substrate and nucleophile concentrations matter in that model. If a product is named with an `R` or `S` descriptor, assign its priorities from the product rather than assuming the letter changes solely from the word “inversion.”

When evaluating a proposed arrow-pushing mechanism, check that arrows begin at electron sources, carbon does not exceed its allowed valence, and charges are consistent with bond changes. An SN1 proposal must include ionization before nucleophile capture; an SN2 proposal must show bond formation and leaving-group departure in the same step. A drawing that puts a free carbocation in the middle of an SN2 sequence describes a different mechanism.

## Connection to biological chemistry

Biological nucleophilic substitutions can build or modify bonds, but an enzyme active site organizes reactants, stabilizes charge, and may transfer protons. That environment is not equivalent to choosing a bulk solvent from a simple SN1/SN2 chart. The general mechanistic questions still help: what is the electron-pair donor, which bond changes, what stabilizes charge, and what evidence supports the proposed sequence? A solution-phase heuristic alone cannot establish a biological mechanism.

## Common errors and limits

- Do not call every reaction with a strong nucleophile SN2; steric access, solvent, leaving group, and competing pathways matter.
- Do not place the nucleophile in the simple SN1 rate law when ionization is the rate-limiting step, or omit it from the simple SN2 law.
- Do not treat all SN1 products as exactly racemic or equate inversion with an automatic `R`/`S` swap.
- Do not assume a favorable substitution pathway excludes E1 or E2. Mechanism comparisons depend on the complete set of reactants and conditions.
- The examples here are conceptual or explicitly synthetic. They are not laboratory procedures, measured kinetics, or recommendations for chemical handling.
