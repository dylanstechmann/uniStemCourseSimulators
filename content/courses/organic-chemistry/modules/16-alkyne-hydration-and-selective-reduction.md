# Alkyne hydration and selective reduction

An alkyne has two π bonds, so it can accept one or two equivalents of hydrogen across its triple bond. Hydration also begins by adding atoms across the π system, but the initially formed enol usually tautomerizes to a carbonyl compound. Regioselectivity and the extent of reduction depend on the reaction model; a product name alone does not establish a procedure or yield.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:

1. Predict the carbonyl products of the two standard hydration routes for a terminal alkyne and explain the enol-to-carbonyl tautomerization.
2. Distinguish partial alkyne reduction to a cis or trans alkene from complete reduction to an alkane.
3. Use formula, degree of unsaturation and hydrogen-equivalent bookkeeping to check a proposed product.

## Hydration makes an enol before a carbonyl

In the standard mercury(II)-catalyzed hydration model, water adds to a terminal alkyne with Markovnikov orientation. The first organic product is an enol, with an OH group on the more substituted carbon of the former triple bond. Tautomerization relocates a proton and the π bond, producing a ketone. Thus, hydration of propyne is represented as the enol CH₃C(OH)=CH₂ followed by acetone (propanone), CH₃COCH₃.

Hydroboration–oxidation provides a complementary orientation for a terminal alkyne. A bulky borane limits a second addition; after oxidation, the enol tautomerizes to an aldehyde. The same three-carbon starting skeleton, propyne, is therefore predicted to give propanal, CH₃CH₂CHO, in this model. The contrast comes from which carbon receives oxygen before tautomerization, not from a different carbon count.

Tautomerization changes the position of a hydrogen and a double bond while conserving atoms. It is not the loss of water. For an internal alkyne, hydration generally gives a ketone after tautomerization; unsymmetrical internal alkynes may present regioselectivity questions beyond this short survey.

## Partial and complete reduction

Addition of one equivalent of H₂ to a C≡C bond reduces one π bond and leaves a C=C bond. The product's geometry depends on the reduction model. A poisoned palladium catalyst such as Lindlar's catalyst gives the cis alkene in the usual syn-addition model. Dissolving-metal reduction with an electron/proton sequence gives the trans alkene in the standard introductory model. These outcomes are not interchangeable: “one equivalent of hydrogen” by itself does not specify which stereoisomer is formed.

Complete reduction adds a second H₂ equivalent and produces an alkane. For 2-butyne, C₄H₆, one equivalent gives a butene, C₄H₈, with one degree of unsaturation remaining; two equivalents give butane, C₄H₁₀, with none. Check both the hydrogen count and the remaining π bond before labeling the reduction partial or complete.

## Worked comparison: hydration of a terminal alkyne

Starting from propyne, compare the carbonyl outcomes rather than assuming that all “hydration” produces the same product:

| Standard route | First C–O placement | Intermediate | Predicted carbonyl |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mercury(II)-catalyzed hydration | Markovnikov | Enol, CH₃C(OH)=CH₂ | Acetone (propanone) |
| Bulky-borane hydroboration–oxidation | Non-Markovnikov | Enol, CH₃CH=CHOH | Propanal |

Each product retains all three carbons and contains one carbonyl after tautomerization. The table states idealized textbook outcomes; it gives no reaction amounts, yields, experimental conditions or handling steps.

## Common errors and limits

- Calling the enol the final isolated product in the standard aqueous hydration model.
- Treating enol–carbonyl tautomerization as dehydration or loss of a carbon atom.
- Assigning the same hydration regioisomer to mercury(II) catalysis and terminal-alkyne hydroboration.
- Calling Lindlar reduction a full reduction; one π bond remains.
- Predicting cis/trans geometry from H₂ equivalents alone without the reduction model.
- Applying terminal-alkyne product rules to every unsymmetrical internal alkyne.
