# Carbonyl chemistry and acyl transfer

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:

1. Explain how carbonyl bonding makes the carbonyl carbon an electrophilic site.
2. Predict whether a nucleophile gives net addition or acyl substitution by checking the groups attached to that carbon.

## Why the carbonyl carbon is electrophilic

In a carbonyl group, oxygen attracts electron density more strongly than carbon. The C=O bond is therefore polarized: the carbonyl carbon is partially positive and oxygen is partially negative. A useful resonance description includes a contributor with greater positive character at carbon and negative character at oxygen. These drawings represent one delocalized electronic structure; atoms do not move between contributors.

The electron-poor carbon can accept an electron pair from a nucleophile. At the same time, the C=O pi electrons move toward oxygen. In a simple addition, the carbonyl carbon changes from trigonal planar, with three sigma-bond directions, to a tetrahedral center with four sigma bonds. Oxygen receives the former pi pair and is often represented as an alkoxide until a proton transfer occurs. Every proposed arrow should begin at an electron pair or bond, and the resulting charges should match the new bonds.

Electrophilicity is a useful predictor, but it is not a complete reaction rule. Steric access, resonance donation from neighboring atoms, the nucleophile’s charge, protonation state, solvent and the availability of a leaving group affect which steps are plausible. Begin by drawing the actual functional group rather than treating every C=O bond as equivalent.

## Addition or substitution?

Nucleophilic addition and nucleophilic acyl substitution share a first step: the nucleophile forms a bond to the carbonyl carbon while the pi electrons shift to oxygen. That produces a tetrahedral intermediate in the electron-accounting model. What happens next depends on the groups attached to the carbonyl carbon.

| Starting group | Typical next step after attack | Net reaction pattern |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Aldehyde or ketone | Proton transfer commonly converts the alkoxide into an alcohol. There is no ordinary heteroatom leaving group on the carbonyl carbon to expel. | Addition: the new nucleophile bond remains, and the carbonyl pi bond becomes a C–O single bond. |
| Carboxylic acid derivative, such as an acid chloride or ester | After suitable proton transfers, the tetrahedral intermediate can collapse: oxygen reforms C=O as a substituent leaves. | Acyl substitution: the incoming group replaces a group already attached to the acyl carbon. |

The tetrahedral intermediate is a mechanistic waypoint, not necessarily an isolable compound. Its formation does not by itself tell you whether the final product is an alcohol or a new carbonyl compound. To decide, inspect the carbonyl carbon’s substituents and ask whether one can leave in the reaction’s protonation context. If no suitable group leaves, simple addition is the relevant net pattern. If a group can depart as the carbonyl reforms, substitution is possible.

## A ketone addition example

Consider the course’s existing example of an organic carbon nucleophile adding to a ketone. The carbon nucleophile supplies an electron pair to the electrophilic carbonyl carbon. The C=O pi pair moves to oxygen, giving a tetrahedral alkoxide with a new carbon–carbon bond. A later proton transfer can give an alcohol. The ketone’s two carbon substituents remain attached to that carbon; neither is expelled as a routine leaving group.

The bookkeeping separates two descriptions. The first arrow-pushing event creates a charged alkoxide intermediate. The net product after proton transfer is an alcohol with the new carbon substituent retained. Do not draw an acyl-substitution collapse unless a plausible leaving group is attached and the mechanism supports its departure. This is a conceptual mechanism example, not a reagent recipe or operating procedure.

## An acyl-substitution example

An acid chloride has a carbonyl carbon bonded to chloride. A neutral amine can add to the polarized carbonyl, shifting the pi pair to oxygen and making a tetrahedral intermediate. Proton transfers can change the amine’s charge and prepare the departing group. As the oxygen pair reforms C=O, the carbon–chloride bond can break and chloride leaves. The net change replaces chloride with the nitrogen-containing group; it is nucleophilic acyl substitution.

The order and exact form of proton-transfer steps depend on the reaction environment. A useful mechanism must account for the nitrogen and oxygen charges at each drawn stage, show where each proton goes, and avoid giving the acyl carbon five bonds. A simplified net equation may summarize reactants and products, but it does not establish the microscopic sequence by itself.

## Why acid chlorides and amides differ

The course question asks why acid chlorides are generally more reactive than amides toward nucleophilic acyl substitution. Two structural effects point in the same direction. Chloride can depart as a comparatively weak base, so it is a better leaving group than a strongly basic amide anion under comparable conditions. In an amide, nitrogen donates electron density into the carbonyl by resonance; this stabilizes the starting derivative and reduces the carbonyl carbon’s electrophilic character. Both effects make the initial attack/collapse pathway less favorable for an ordinary amide than for an acid chloride.

This is a qualitative comparison, not a guarantee that every acid chloride reacts faster than every amide under arbitrary conditions or that amides never react. Protonation, activation, solvent, steric environment and the identity of the nucleophile can change the pathway. A practical reactivity ranking should identify the class of derivatives and the stated context. Biological systems often use activation and catalysis to make an otherwise slow acyl transfer selective.

## Connect the mechanism to biomolecules

Acyl transfer is important in biological chemistry because an acyl group can move between a donor and an acceptor. Enzymes can position nucleophiles, provide proton donors or acceptors, and stabilize charged intermediates. Activated donors such as thioesters can participate in transfer chemistry, while peptide bonds are amides whose stability and reactivity depend strongly on their environment and catalysis. The same structural questions remain useful: which atom donates the electron pair, what substituent can leave, and what proton or charge changes accompany collapse?

A biological role does not make a solution-phase rule automatically predictive. The presence of an amide bond alone does not establish spontaneous cleavage; observed bond turnover requires chemical and biological context. These examples explain functional-group reasoning and make no medical or laboratory recommendation.

## Common errors and limits

- Do not say that every nucleophile–carbonyl encounter is substitution. Aldehydes and ketones usually lack the leaving group needed for ordinary acyl substitution.
- Do not call every reaction with an acid derivative simple addition. If a tetrahedral intermediate collapses and expels a substituent as the carbonyl reforms, the net process is substitution.
- Do not omit the C=O pi-electron shift when the nucleophile forms a new bond to the carbonyl carbon.
- Do not ignore formal charge or proton transfer. A neutral amine attack and an anionic nucleophile attack produce different charge bookkeeping.
- Do not rank derivatives by leaving-group ability alone; resonance donation and the reaction context also matter.
- The examples describe conceptual electron flow, not measured rates, synthetic instructions or chemical-handling guidance.
