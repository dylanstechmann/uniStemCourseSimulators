# Benzyne and mechanism evidence: eliminate, add and preserve the atom map

**Status:** original ungraded instruction developed with substantial AI assistance. The ring maps and branching values are paper models. This reading extends the aromatic-substitution lesson, which develops addition–elimination on an activated ring, with a different sequence. It supplies no experimental procedure, operating conditions or prediction of an actual product ratio.

## Similar net substitutions can have different intermediates

A net replacement of an aryl leaving group by a nucleophile does not identify the mechanism. In the activated addition–elimination pathway developed earlier, the nucleophile first adds to the aromatic ring and forms an anionic intermediate; loss of the leaving group restores aromaticity. An appropriate electron-withdrawing substituent can stabilize that intermediate through its conjugated positions.

In a common benzyne-forming elimination–addition pathway, loss of a leaving group and a neighboring hydrogen first creates an aryne intermediate. A nucleophile then adds to that intermediate, followed by protonation. The order of bond changes differs even when a net product drawing looks like substitution. Do not call this ordinary SN2 attack at an aryl carbon; the geometry and reaction sequence are different.

The textbook leaving-group/ortho-hydrogen route is one route to an aryne, not a statement that every aryne precursor in chemistry must have those groups. This lesson restricts its formation rule explicitly. Other precursors and mechanisms are outside the exercise.

## The carbon skeleton stays traceable

Number a six-carbon ring C1–C2–C3–C4–C5–C6–C1. Put the leaving group on C1. Under the stipulated dehydrohalogenation model, an adjacent ring carbon must supply the hydrogen removed with the leaving group. C2 and C6 are the adjacent candidates; C3 is not ortho to C1.

For an unsubstituted halobenzene formula C6H5X, formal loss of HX leaves C6H4. Relative to benzene C6H6, the intermediate has one extra degree of unsaturation. It is often drawn with an additional bond between the neighboring ring carbons, but the constrained ring geometry makes it very different from an unconstrained straight alkyne. The additional overlap is strongly distorted. Do not assume ordinary linear-alkyne geometry or count the extra bond as a second independent aromatic circuit.

The ring carbon labels do not exchange places just because the intermediate is reactive. A product can place the incoming substituent on either end of the aryne bond while preserving the original carbon identities. This positional outcome is different from a rearrangement that moves the carbon skeleton itself.

## Follow charge and hydrogen explicitly

For a declared aryne bond C1–C6, a negative nitrogen nucleophile can bond to C1 while the additional bond electrons give a carbanionic center at C6. Protonation of C6 completes that addition path. Alternatively the nucleophile can bond to C6, leaving a carbanionic center at C1 that is then protonated. The final placement of the new C–N bond distinguishes the paths.

Using NH2− followed by H+ as a formula exercise, C6H4 gains one N and three total H to give C6H7N. The final nitrogen is bonded to one ring carbon and two hydrogens; the extra proton terminates the carbon-anion step. Counting only the two nitrogen hydrogens misses the separate protonation of carbon.

A degree-of-unsaturation formula that includes nitrogen is DBE=(2C+2+N−H−X)/2 for an appropriate neutral closed-shell formula. Benzyne C6H4 has DBE five; the stated final C6H7N has DBE four. This formula check is consistent with addition across the extra bond, but it does not by itself prove the intermediate or identify every product.

## An original blocked-ring map

Use the numbered ring above with a leaving group at C1, an inert substituent occupying C2's hydrogen site, and a paper carbon tag at C3. All remaining required positions carry their declared hydrogens. Restrict the formation rule to removing an ortho hydrogen from the starting aryl leaving-group substrate.

C2 has no such hydrogen, so the permitted aryne bond is C1–C6. Attack at C1 leaves the tag two ring edges from the new C–N bond, a meta relationship. Attack at C6 leaves the same C3 tag three edges away, a para relationship. Track original labels first, then renumber products only if a name requires it. Renumbering a product must not be mistaken for physical movement of the tag.

Both atom maps are possible under the stipulated intermediate. Their existence does not supply a 1:1 ratio. Substitution, geometry and competing rates can affect branching. If a question supplies a 60% C1-attack fraction, that fraction is a separate invented model input, not something deduced from drawing two arrows.

## Mechanism evidence should test alternatives

A positional tag distribution consistent with attack at two aryne carbons can support an elimination–addition proposal, especially when compared with a mechanism predicting only attachment at the original leaving-group carbon. It is not a universal uniqueness proof. Other rearrangements, parallel pathways, isotope effects, side products or observation errors can matter in a real study.

Similarly, a trapped intermediate-like product is evidence whose interpretation depends on the trap's selectivity and the alternative routes that could form it. A product formula alone cannot distinguish constitutional isomers. An experiment should identify which atom or bond is observed and which alternatives are excluded rather than equating a plausible arrow scheme with an observed mechanism.

The earlier activated-SNAr lesson supplies another useful contrast: its anionic addition intermediate is stabilized by appropriate ring substitution, while this exercise stipulates elimination before nucleophilic addition. The same nucleophile and a ring leaving group do not erase that distinction. Absence of an ortho hydrogen blocks the declared elimination route, but does not prove the molecule is inert under every imaginable chemistry.

## Rehearsal and reporting

For a separate map, place the blocking substituent at C6 and the paper tag at C4, with the leaving group still at C1. Identify the allowed aryne edge and the tag's relationship to incoming nitrogen for attack at each end. Give a text atom map as well as any drawing.

For a supplied 70% attack fraction at C1 and 30% at the other end, compute tag-position fractions conditional on complete conversion and no other pathways. Explain what changes if conversion is only 80% and the remaining material is unreacted. Branching among products and the fraction of the initial inventory converted to one product are different denominators.
