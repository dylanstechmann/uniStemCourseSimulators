# Elimination, alkenes, and stereochemical control

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:

1. Distinguish E1 and E2 pathways from the order of bond changes, substrate and conditions.
2. Predict plausible alkene products while stating the assumptions behind any regioselectivity claim.

## From a saturated carbon to an alkene

An elimination removes a leaving group from one carbon and a hydrogen from an adjacent carbon, called a beta carbon. The electrons from the carbon–hydrogen bond form a carbon–carbon pi bond as the leaving group departs. The carbon bearing the leaving group is the alpha carbon. Before drawing a product, identify the alpha carbon, list each adjacent beta carbon with at least one removable hydrogen, and check which new double bond each choice would create. This inventory catches a common error: drawing an alkene at a carbon that is not adjacent to the leaving group or has no beta hydrogen.

Two common pathways differ in the timing of those bond changes. In E2, a base removes a beta proton while the C–H bond electrons form the pi bond and the C–leaving-group bond breaks in one concerted step. There is no carbocation intermediate. The simple rate law is rate = k[substrate][base], because the substrate and base participate in the rate-determining event. A strong base and an accessible beta hydrogen commonly support this pathway, although substrate, solvent, leaving-group ability and competing reactions still matter.

In E1, the leaving group first departs to produce a carbocation; a base then removes a beta proton to form the alkene. For the simple model in which ionization is rate-limiting, rate = k[substrate]. The base is not in that slow step. Carbocation stability therefore influences the pathway, and rearrangement can be possible before deprotonation. Because E1 and SN1 share an ionization step, both substitution and elimination products can form under conditions that support either capture of the cation or loss of a beta proton. Product mixtures are a reason to describe a pathway as favored, not exclusive.

E1cB is a third, distinct sequence: base removes a proton first to form a carbanion-like intermediate, then a leaving group departs. It is especially relevant when the anion is stabilized, for example by a nearby carbonyl, and the leaving group is poor. Do not treat E1cB as a synonym for E1: the order and identity of the intermediate differ.

## Compare mechanism evidence

| Evidence | E2 tendency | E1 tendency |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Bond changes | Beta C–H cleavage, pi-bond formation and leaving-group departure are concerted. | Leaving-group departure gives a carbocation before beta deprotonation. |
| Simple rate model | rate = k[substrate][base]. | rate = k[substrate] if ionization is the rate-limiting step. |
| Conditions and substrate | A sufficiently strong base can drive elimination; crowded substrates can disfavor substitution and still undergo E2. | A substrate able to form a relatively stabilized carbocation and an ion-stabilizing medium can support ionization. |
| Geometry | The reacting beta C–H and C–leaving-group bonds are usually aligned anti-periplanar. | No corresponding anti-periplanar requirement applies to the two separate bond-breaking steps. |
| Competing pathway | Substitution may compete, especially when nucleophilic attack is accessible. | SN1 capture commonly competes after the shared carbocation-forming step. |

These entries are evidence to combine, not a decision table that guarantees a unique product. A secondary substrate, for example, may give different proportions as base strength, solvent, temperature, or steric demand changes. Identify what is stipulated and avoid inventing a condition that the problem does not supply.

## Geometry controls E2

E2 needs an orbital arrangement that lets the breaking C–H bond and departing C–leaving-group bond support formation of the new pi bond. The usual productive arrangement is anti-periplanar: the four atoms involved lie in one plane, with the beta hydrogen and leaving group pointing in opposite directions in a Newman-style view. This is a stereochemical requirement of the concerted step, not a temporary carbocation rotating into a better orientation.

In an acyclic molecule, rotation about a single bond may allow an anti-periplanar conformation to be reached. In a cyclohexane chair, the corresponding arrangement is trans-diaxial: the leaving group and a beta hydrogen on a neighboring carbon must both be axial and point oppositely. A ring flip exchanges axial and equatorial positions but preserves each substituent’s up/down orientation. If the leaving group is equatorial in one chair, a ring flip may expose the axial arrangement; the flipped chair can be less populated when large substituents become axial. Product prediction therefore depends on whether the required conformation exists and is accessible.

The geometry can override a simple substitution-count rule. If only one adjacent axial hydrogen is anti to the leaving group, E2 is constrained to the alkene formed from that hydrogen—even if another possible alkene would be more substituted. Check the chair before selecting the product. Do not apply an acyclic “most substituted” shortcut before verifying that the required bonds can align.

## Regiochemistry and alkene products

If beta hydrogens are available at more than one adjacent carbon, elimination can produce constitutional isomers with the double bond in different positions. Zaitsev selectivity is a useful tendency: in many base-induced eliminations, the more substituted and often more stable alkene is favored. It is not an absolute rule. A bulky base, a restricted conformation, a leaving group’s placement, or another competing process can favor a less substituted product. State “assuming Zaitsev selectivity” when the problem gives that assumption, as the practice item here does.

For acid-catalyzed dehydration of 2-methyl-2-butanol through a carbocation, removal of a proton from either of two non-equivalent beta-carbon types can form 2-methyl-2-butene or 2-methyl-1-butene. Under the stated Zaitsev assumption, 2-methyl-2-butene is the expected major alkene because it is more substituted. The same product prediction should not be transferred to every base, substrate conformation, or elimination mechanism without checking the conditions.

Regiochemistry answers where the double bond forms; alkene geometry answers the arrangement around that double bond. When each alkene carbon bears two different substituents, E/Z stereoisomers may be possible. A concerted E2 reaction preserves the relationship encoded by the reacting conformation, so draw or inspect the beta hydrogen and leaving-group alignment before assigning alkene geometry. If a question asks only for the constitutional product or ignores double-bond stereochemistry, do not add unsupported E/Z claims.

## A disciplined product check

For a proposed elimination:

1. Mark the alpha carbon and enumerate its adjacent beta carbons and beta hydrogens.
2. Decide whether the prompt supports E1, E2 or another pathway; record which assumptions you used.
3. For E2, inspect anti-periplanar alignment, including trans-diaxial geometry for a chair.
4. Draw each alkene allowed by the mechanism before applying a stated regioselectivity tendency.
5. Check atom count, charge and bond order; then distinguish constitutional products from possible E/Z stereoisomers.

For the cyclohexane practice item, the key phrase is “typical concerted E2.” It requires an anti-periplanar C–H bond and C–leaving-group bond, which corresponds to trans-diaxial alignment in a chair. A carbocation rotation is not available in this mechanism. For the dehydration practice item, the prompt explicitly supplies both a carbocation pathway and Zaitsev selectivity, so the more substituted alkene is selected. The preserved answer keys therefore depend on different kinds of evidence: geometry in one question and a stated product-distribution assumption in the other.

## Biological and model limits

Biological eliminations can be enzyme-directed and may use proton transfers, leaving groups, or stabilized intermediates that do not match a simple alkyl-halide chart. E1cB-like steps are particularly relevant when a carbonyl stabilizes an intermediate anion. A biological mechanism still requires evidence about the substrate, catalyst and reaction sequence; analogy alone is not proof.

The rate laws above are simplified kinetic models, not fitted measurements. The product examples are conceptual and the numerical proportions sometimes quoted in reference texts are not reused as course data. This lesson offers no synthesis protocol or chemical-handling recommendation.
