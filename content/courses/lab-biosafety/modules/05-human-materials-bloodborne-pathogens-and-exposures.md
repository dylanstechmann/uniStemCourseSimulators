# Human cells and tissues, bloodborne pathogens and exposure response

Stem-cell, regenerative-medicine and aging laboratories work with human blood, tissue and cell lines every day. These materials can carry pathogens nobody has tested for. This lesson combines two US federal sources, adapted with attribution. Appendix H of BMBL, 6th edition (CDC and NIH, 2020) sets out how to assess the risk of human and other primate cells. The OSHA Bloodborne Pathogens Standard, 29 CFR 1910.1030, states what employers must provide when workers are exposed to human blood and other potentially infectious materials. The lesson ends with what to do in the first minutes after an exposure.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:

1. Assess the relative risk of human, non-human primate and other mammalian cell material from its source, cell type and culture type, and state the minimum containment for human and primate cells.
2. Explain universal precautions and identify materials covered by the Bloodborne Pathogens Standard.
3. List what the standard requires of employers: the exposure control plan, engineering and work-practice controls, PPE, hepatitis B vaccination, training and records, with their timing.
4. Carry out the immediate response to a needlestick, splash or cut, and explain why reporting it at once matters.

## Risk from human and primate material

BMBL Appendix H says work with eukaryotic cell cultures is generally low-risk, but that risk rises with human and other primate cells and with primary cells. A risk assessment considers three things.

- **Source.** The closer the species is to humans, the higher the risk, because pathogens are often species-specific. Old World primate material, such as macaque tissue, may carry Macacine herpesvirus 1 (herpes B), which is usually mild in macaques but can be fatal in humans, so it is always treated as potentially infected.
- **Cell or tissue type.** Hematopoietic and lymphoid cells can have tumorigenic potential and carry more risk. Epithelial cells and fibroblasts carry the least.
- **Culture type.** Primary cells, taken straight from tissue, have the highest chance of carrying undetected pathogens. Continuous lines can be better characterized, but lines immortalized with viral agents such as EBV or SV40 carry viral genetic material, and permissive lines can support viral contamination. BMBL notes a reported case of tumour development after an accidental needlestick.

Recombinant material expressed by a line, pathogens added on purpose, and agents picked up by passage through animals all change the assessment. BMBL's minimum is clear: **human and other primate cells are treated as potentially infectious and handled with BSL-2 practices, engineering controls and facilities**, with culturing done in a biological safety cabinet. Waste culture material is decontaminated before disposal. Staff working with these cells should be in an occupational medicine program for bloodborne pathogens.

## Universal precautions and what the standard covers

The OSHA standard applies to occupational exposure to blood and **other potentially infectious materials (OPIM)**. BMBL notes that OPIM includes body fluids, tissues and primary cell lines. Its central rule, paragraph (d)(1), is **universal precautions**: all such materials are handled as if infectious, and where body fluid types are hard to tell apart, all body fluids are treated as potentially infectious. Nobody decides that a sample "looks safe".

## What the employer must provide

| Requirement | What 29 CFR 1910.1030 says (paraphrased) |
| --- | --- |
| Exposure Control Plan, (c)(1) | A written plan to eliminate or minimize exposure, accessible to employees, reviewed and updated at least annually and when tasks change, documenting consideration of safer devices |
| Engineering and work-practice controls, (d)(2) | Hand washing at once after removing gloves and after skin contact; contaminated needles and sharps not bent, recapped or removed unless there is no feasible alternative, and never sheared or broken; no eating, drinking, cosmetics or contact-lens handling where exposure is likely; no mouth pipetting |
| Sharps disposal, (d)(4)(iii)(A) | Contaminated sharps discarded at once in containers that are closable, puncture-resistant, leak-proof on the sides and bottom, and labelled or colour-coded |
| Personal protective equipment, (d)(3) | Provided, cleaned, repaired and replaced at no cost to the employee; garments penetrated by blood removed at once |
| Decontamination, (d)(4)(ii)(A) | Work surfaces decontaminated after procedures and immediately after overt contamination or spills |
| Hepatitis B vaccination, (f) | Made available after training and within 10 working days of initial assignment to all occupationally exposed employees |
| Post-exposure evaluation, (f)(3) | After a reported exposure incident, a confidential medical evaluation and follow-up made available immediately |
| Training, (g)(2) | At initial assignment and at least annually thereafter |
| Records, (h) | Medical records kept for the duration of employment plus 30 years; training records for 3 years |

Research laboratories and production facilities working with HIV and HBV have additional requirements in paragraph (e), including a biosafety manual reviewed at least annually and BSCs certified when installed, when moved and at least annually.

## The first minutes after an exposure

Exposures are emergencies with a short window for action. The standard requires employees to wash skin with soap and water, or flush mucous membranes with water, immediately or as soon as feasible after contact. A typical laboratory sequence:

1. **Stop and decontaminate.** Needlestick or cut: wash the wound with soap and water. Eye splash: flush at the eyewash for the time set by your institution's procedure. Mouth or nose: rinse with water. Follow your occupational health service's instructions for anything beyond this first wash.
2. **Make the work safe.** Cap or contain the material if you can do so quickly, then leave the work area.
3. **Report immediately** to your supervisor and the occupational health service, following your laboratory's exposure procedure. The standard makes a confidential medical evaluation available immediately after a reported exposure, including post-exposure prophylaxis when medically indicated as recommended by the U.S. Public Health Service. That care is time-sensitive, which is why reporting cannot wait until the end of the day.
4. **Record** what happened: the material, the agent if known, the procedure and the route.
5. **Follow up** with the medical evaluation, and help review the incident so the risk assessment can be updated.

## Common mistakes

- Treating a "screened" or commercially sourced human cell line as free of risk.
- Culturing primary human cells on an open bench.
- Recapping a needle by hand after drawing blood.
- Finishing the experiment before reporting a needlestick.
- Assuming the hepatitis B vaccine is optional for the employer to offer to exposed staff.

## Worked example

**Problem.** A researcher starts a project deriving induced pluripotent stem cells from donor blood. List the risk factors and the controls required.

**Step 1: risk factors.** Human source; primary blood cells, which BMBL notes are hematopoietic with possible tumorigenic potential; donors whose infection status may be unknown; reprogramming vectors that may be viral.

**Step 2: containment.** BSL-2 practices and facilities at a minimum, with all open handling in a Class II cabinet. Viral vectors add to the risk assessment and need Institutional Biosafety Committee review.

**Step 3: OSHA program.** The researcher is covered by the exposure control plan, trained before starting and annually, offered hepatitis B vaccination within 10 working days of assignment, and given sharps containers at the point of use.

**Step 4: exposure readiness.** The researcher knows where the eyewash is, the exposure procedure and whom to call, and reports any incident at once.

## Limits of this lesson

The OSHA requirements are paraphrased for teaching. The regulation's text, your employer's exposure control plan and your occupational health service govern real obligations. Post-exposure medical care is not described beyond the first steps, and nothing here is medical advice. Requirements outside the United States differ.
