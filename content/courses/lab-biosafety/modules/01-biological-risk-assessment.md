# Biological risk assessment

Every biosafety decision, from the containment level of a room to the gloves on a bench, should come out of a risk assessment. Biosafety manuals describe practices, but only an assessment of a particular agent, in a particular procedure, done by particular people, says which practices are needed. This lesson follows the six-step process set out in Section II of *Biosafety in Microbiological and Biomedical Laboratories* (BMBL, 6th edition, CDC and NIH, 2020), a US federal work whose guidance is adapted here with attribution.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:

1. Distinguish a hazard from a risk, and rank tasks with a qualitative probability × consequence score.
2. Identify agent hazards and procedure hazards, including the routes by which laboratory-associated infections occur.
3. Apply the hierarchy of controls, distinguishing inherent risk from residual risk.
4. Carry out the six steps of the BMBL risk management process, including who reviews the assessment and when it is revisited.

## Hazard and risk

BMBL defines **hazards** as substances or situations capable of causing adverse effects to health or safety, and **risk** as what arises when people interact with hazards: a function of both the probability of an adverse event and its consequences. A culture of a pathogen in a closed, labelled tube in a freezer is a hazard with little risk; the same culture being vortexed on an open bench is a risk.

Biological risk assessment is usually qualitative. A common working method scores probability and consequence each from 1 (lowest) to 5 (highest) and multiplies them to rank tasks. BMBL notes that the product of the probability and consequence estimates gives a relative value for prioritizing risks. The numbers rank tasks against each other; they are not measurements.

| Task (synthetic example) | Probability | Consequence | Score |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Pipetting a BSL-2 culture in a biosafety cabinet | 2 | 3 | 6 |
| Centrifuging the culture in unsealed buckets | 4 | 3 | 12 |
| Passaging a well-characterized non-human cell line | 1 | 1 | 1 |

The centrifugation scores highest and is addressed first. Sealed rotors or safety cups loaded and unloaded in the cabinet lower its probability score.

## Step 1: agent hazards and inherent risk

Start with the agent and estimate the **inherent risk**, the risk with no mitigation in place. BMBL lists the agent characteristics to consider: its capability to infect and cause disease in a susceptible host, the severity of disease, the availability of preventive measures and effective treatments, the routes of transmission, the infectious dose, stability in the environment, host range, whether it is indigenous or exotic, and its genetic characteristics. For a genetically modified agent, ask the same questions of the wild type, then ask whether the modification could change pathogenicity or treatment susceptibility.

Sources include the agent summary statements in BMBL Section VIII and the Public Health Agency of Canada's Pathogen Safety Data Sheets. The NIH Guidelines assign agents to four **Risk Groups**, based on the severity of the disease they cause and the availability of interventions. BMBL stresses that Risk Groups correlate with, but do not equate to, Biosafety Levels: the Risk Group is a starting estimate, and the procedure can raise or lower what is needed.

## Step 2: procedure hazards

BMBL names the principal procedure hazards: agent concentration, suspension volume, equipment and procedures that generate small-particle aerosols and droplets, and the use of sharps. Investigations of laboratory-associated infections have found five routes of transmission:

1. parenteral inoculation with syringe needles or other contaminated sharps;
2. spills and splashes onto skin and mucous membranes;
3. ingestion through mouth pipetting;
4. animal bites and scratches;
5. inhalation of infectious aerosols.

The first four are easy to detect, yet in the retrospective review BMBL cites they accounted for less than 20% of reported infections; many have no known source. Aerosols are the probable cause of many of the rest, because any procedure that puts energy into a suspension makes them: pipetting, blending, centrifuging, sonicating, vortexing and cell sorting among others.

Concentration and volume multiply the hazard. In a synthetic illustration, if a procedure releases a fixed fraction of 10⁻⁶ of a 1 mL suspension as respirable aerosol, a culture at 10⁹ organisms per mL releases about 1000 organisms, while a sample at 10³ per mL releases about 0.001, that is, almost certainly none. The same procedure can be trivial or serious depending on what is in the tube.

## Step 3: choose the Biosafety Level and additional precautions

With agent and procedure hazards assessed, choose the Biosafety Level and any extra precautions. BMBL asks risk management to follow a **hierarchy of controls**: first eliminate or reduce the hazard (an attenuated strain, a smaller volume, inactivated material), then apply engineering controls (a biosafety cabinet, sealed rotors, safety-engineered sharps) and administrative controls (training, procedures, restricted access), and only then add personal protective equipment for what remains. Controls are layered so that one failure does not lead to exposure.

What is left after the controls is the **residual risk**, which must be brought to a level the institution accepts. In the table above, sealed centrifuge safety cups opened only in a cabinet might lower the probability score from 4 to 2, giving a residual score of 6. BMBL also warns against over-control: safeguards far beyond the need add cost and burden with little gain. Where information is insufficient, it recommends considering additional safeguards until more data are available.

## Steps 4–6: review, proficiency and revisiting

4. **Review before implementing.** Review the assessment and the chosen safeguards with a biosafety professional, a subject matter expert and the Institutional Biosafety Committee or its equivalent.
5. **Evaluate proficiency and equipment.** As an ongoing process, check that staff are proficient in the safe practices and that safety equipment works: cabinets certified, centrifuge cups intact, training current. Close any training or competency gaps.
6. **Revisit and verify.** Review the strategies regularly and whenever procedures, equipment, agents or people change, and update the biosafety manual and SOPs to match.

Throughout, BMBL emphasizes **risk communication**: the people doing the work should help make the assessment and understand its conclusions, which is part of a positive culture of safety.

## Common mistakes

- Equating an agent's Risk Group with the required Biosafety Level, without assessing the procedure.
- Treating the score as a measurement rather than a ranking.
- Starting with PPE instead of eliminating or engineering out the hazard.
- Writing the assessment once and never revisiting it after the protocol changes.
- Assuming that an infection without an obvious incident could not have come from the laboratory; most have no identified source.

## Worked example

**Problem.** A laboratory will grow a BSL-2 bacterium to high density (10¹⁰ CFU/mL, 50 mL), concentrate it by centrifugation and sonicate it to make lysates. Identify the main hazards and propose controls in hierarchy order.

**Step 1: agent.** A moderate-hazard pathogen, treatable, with possible aerosol transmission (consult its agent summary statement).

**Step 2: procedure.** High concentration and a large volume; centrifugation and sonication both generate aerosols.

**Step 3: controls.** Reduce the hazard first: grow only the volume needed. Engineering: sealed rotors or safety cups loaded and opened in a Class II cabinet, and sonication inside the cabinet or in a sealed vessel. Administrative: a written SOP, training, and restricted access during the work. PPE: coat, gloves and eye protection, and respiratory protection if the assessment calls for it.

**Steps 4–6.** Review with the biosafety officer and the Institutional Biosafety Committee before starting, confirm cabinet certification and centrifuge cup integrity, and revisit the assessment if the strain or scale changes.

## Limits of this lesson

The probability and consequence scores and the aerosol-release fraction are synthetic illustrations, not measured values. Risk assessment for a real agent needs its agent summary statement, institutional policy and review by qualified biosafety professionals; this lesson does not replace them.
