# Chemical hygiene, hazard communication and transport of infectious substances

Biological laboratories are also chemical laboratories: fixatives, solvents, acids, stains and disinfectants all carry their own hazards. Samples also travel between laboratories, and that is regulated too. This lesson covers three US federal sources, adapted with attribution: the OSHA Laboratory Standard (29 CFR 1910.1450), the OSHA Hazard Communication Standard (29 CFR 1910.1200) and Appendix C of BMBL, 6th edition, on transporting infectious substances.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:

1. State what a Chemical Hygiene Plan must contain under the OSHA Laboratory Standard, who is responsible for it, and how often it is reviewed.
2. Read a hazard-communication label, naming its six elements and the meaning of the two signal words.
3. Find information quickly on a 16-section safety data sheet.
4. Distinguish Category A and Category B infectious substances, and describe triple packaging and the training required before anyone ships one.

## The Chemical Hygiene Plan

Where hazardous chemicals are used in a laboratory, the OSHA Laboratory Standard requires the employer to develop and carry out a written **Chemical Hygiene Plan** (CHP) that protects employees from the health hazards of the chemicals they use. Among its required elements are:

- standard operating procedures for work with hazardous chemicals;
- criteria for using control measures such as fume hoods and PPE;
- a requirement that **fume hoods and other protective equipment function properly**, with specific measures to ensure it;
- provisions for employee information and training;
- the circumstances requiring prior approval before a procedure;
- provisions for medical consultation and examination;
- designation of the people responsible, including a **Chemical Hygiene Officer**;
- additional protection for particularly hazardous substances, such as select carcinogens, reproductive toxins and highly toxic chemicals.

The employer reviews the CHP's effectiveness **at least annually** and updates it as needed. Employees must have information and training at their **initial assignment** to a work area with hazardous chemicals and before assignments involving new exposures, and they must be told where reference material such as safety data sheets is kept. Labels on incoming containers must not be removed or defaced. Exposures to OSHA-regulated substances must not exceed their permissible exposure limits, and exposure is monitored where there is reason to believe it routinely exceeds the action level.

## Reading a label

Under the Hazard Communication Standard, a shipped container of a hazardous chemical carries six label elements:

1. product identifier;
2. **signal word**: "Danger" for the more severe hazards, "Warning" for the less severe;
3. hazard statement(s), describing the nature of the hazard;
4. pictogram(s);
5. precautionary statement(s), describing how to prevent or respond to exposure;
6. name, US address and telephone number of the manufacturer, importer or other responsible party.

The signal word, hazard statements and pictograms must appear together on the label. When you decant a chemical into a secondary container, your laboratory's labelling procedure applies. An unlabelled bottle of clear liquid is an incident waiting to happen.

## Using a safety data sheet

Employers must have a safety data sheet (SDS) for each hazardous chemical used. The standard fixes 16 sections in a set order, which makes an SDS fast to search once you know the layout:

| Section | Heading | Look here for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Identification | product, supplier, emergency phone |
| 2 | Hazard(s) identification | classification, signal word, pictograms |
| 3 | Composition/information on ingredients | what is in a mixture |
| 4 | First-aid measures | what to do after exposure |
| 5 | Fire-fighting measures | suitable extinguishers |
| 6 | Accidental release measures | spill response |
| 7 | Handling and storage | incompatibilities, storage conditions |
| 8 | Exposure controls/personal protection | exposure limits, ventilation, gloves and eye protection |
| 9 | Physical and chemical properties | flash point, vapour pressure |
| 10 | Stability and reactivity | reactions to avoid |
| 11 | Toxicological information | health effects |
| 12–15 | Ecological, disposal, transport, regulatory | (OSHA does not enforce 12–15) |
| 16 | Other information | date of preparation or last revision |

Before using a chemical for the first time, read sections 2, 4, 7 and 8 at least, and check section 16 to see how current the sheet is.

## Shipping infectious substances

BMBL Appendix C explains that infectious substances are regulated as hazardous materials in transport, by the US Department of Transportation and, for air, under ICAO and the IATA Dangerous Goods Regulations. Shippers and carriers must be **trained** before they prepare a shipment.

- **Category A** (UN 2814, affecting humans; UN 2900, affecting animals only): substances transported in a form capable of causing permanent disability or life-threatening or fatal disease in otherwise healthy humans or animals if exposure occurs.
- **Category B** (UN 3373, "Biological substance, Category B"): infectious substances that do not meet the Category A criteria.

Both use **triple packaging**: a leak-proof (Category A: watertight) primary receptacle, leak-proof secondary packaging, and rigid outer packaging. For liquids, the secondary packaging holds enough absorbent material for the entire contents of all primary receptacles, and for Category A a list of contents is placed on or near the secondary packaging. Category A packages must pass drop and water-spray tests, withstand a pressure differential of at least 95 kPa and temperatures from −40 °C to +55 °C, and carry a Division 6.2 label with shipping papers and emergency response information. For air transport, Category B primary or secondary packaging must also withstand a 95 kPa pressure differential.

Classifying a material, choosing packaging and marking a package are tasks for trained, certified shippers working under their institution's procedures. This lesson explains what the categories and packaging layers are for. It does not make anyone qualified to ship.

## Common mistakes

- Treating the CHP as a binder on a shelf rather than the procedures you follow.
- Using a fume hood without checking its flow indicator or certification sticker.
- Decanting a reagent into an unlabelled bottle.
- Reading only the pictogram and skipping the hazard and precautionary statements.
- Packing a frozen sample for courier pick-up without being trained and certified to ship.

## Worked example

**Problem.** You are about to make a 4% formaldehyde fixative from paraformaldehyde for the first time, and a collaborator asks you to send them some fixed tissue and some live virus-transduced cells. What do you check, and what do you hand off?

**Step 1: the chemical.** Read the SDS: section 2 for the hazards (formaldehyde is classified as a carcinogen), section 8 for ventilation and gloves, section 7 for storage, section 4 for first aid. Work in a chemical fume hood whose flow indicator reads normal. Follow the CHP's provisions for particularly hazardous substances, which may require prior approval.

**Step 2: the label.** Label the fixative bottle with its contents and hazards following your laboratory's procedure.

**Step 3: the shipment.** Live transduced human cells may be infectious substances and need classification by a trained shipper; adequately fixed tissue may be classified differently. Hand both to your institution's trained shipping personnel instead of packing them yourself.

## Limits of this lesson

The regulatory requirements are paraphrased for teaching; the regulation text, your employer's CHP and hazard-communication program, and certified shipping personnel govern real work. GHS hazard classification criteria, the pictogram set, exemptions in the transport regulations and requirements outside the United States are not covered.
