# Heat transfer and coupled transport

Heat transfer follows temperature differences, but the physical route matters. A stationary solid can conduct energy through its internal temperature gradient. A moving fluid can carry energy with its motion and exchange it with a surface by convection. Any surface above absolute zero emits thermal radiation; two surfaces can exchange it even when a vacuum separates them. A useful model names the mechanism in each region and states what is held fixed before combining the rates.

## Three mechanisms and their rate laws

Fourier's law describes conduction. In one dimension, the conductive heat flux in the positive x direction is q''x = −k dT/dx, where k is thermal conductivity in W/(m·K). The minus sign makes heat flow toward lower temperature. Flux is a rate per area in W/m²; multiplying by area gives a heat-transfer rate q̇ in W. For a plane, uniform slab of thickness L and area A, with constant k and no internal generation, integration gives q̇ = (T1−T2)/Rcond, where Rcond = L/(kA) in K/W. This expression assumes approximately one-dimensional steady conduction, negligible edge effects, and the same area throughout the layer. A thin high-conductivity layer has a small resistance; a thick low-conductivity layer has a large one.

Convection is energy exchange between a surface and a moving fluid. The surface heat rate is q̇conv = hA(Ts−T∞), where Ts is surface temperature, T∞ is the representative fluid temperature away from the surface, and h is the convective heat-transfer coefficient in W/(m²·K). For a fixed h and area this can be written with Rconv = 1/(hA). Unlike k, h is not only a material property: flow speed, fluid properties, geometry, and boundary-layer development affect it. A correlation for h therefore carries the assumptions and range of its flow and geometry. Motion transports warm or cool fluid, while the thin region next to the surface still has a temperature gradient.

Thermal radiation is electromagnetic energy emitted by surfaces. For a small gray surface exchanging with a much larger isothermal surrounding, a useful net-rate approximation is q̇rad = εσA(Ts⁴−Tsur⁴), with temperatures in kelvin, emissivity ε between zero and one, and Stefan–Boltzmann constant σ = 5.670×10⁻⁸ W/(m²·K⁴). The fourth powers matter: using Celsius in this equation gives the wrong result. If a resistance form is convenient, factor the difference of fourth powers and define a local linearized coefficient hrad = εσ(Ts+Tsur)(Ts²+Tsur²). Then q̇rad = hrad A(Ts−Tsur), and Rrad = 1/(hrad A). This is a local approximation because hrad changes with the temperatures. More complex surface shapes require view factors and exchange among multiple surfaces; the single-surrounding expression should not be used as if every enclosure were a large black surface.

These mechanisms can occur in sequence. Consider moving fluid, a solid wall, a vacuum gap, and a colder surface on the far side. Convection connects the fluid to the wall, conduction crosses the wall, and radiation crosses the gap. There is no convection across an evacuated gap because it contains no moving material, and there is no conductive path through an ideal vacuum. In a real chamber the surface may also exchange radiation with other objects, and residual gas may conduct some heat, so the idealized sequence must match the physical setup. Within a biological aqueous system at moderate temperatures, conduction and convection are often central; radiation may be smaller, but the comparison depends on area, temperatures, emissivity, and the value of h. Do not decide that a mechanism is negligible by its name alone.

## Resistance networks for steady heat flow

For steady one-dimensional layers in series, the same heat rate passes through each layer and the temperature drops add. Thus Rtotal = R1 + R2 + … and q̇ = ΔT/Rtotal. A contact between imperfectly joined solids may add contact resistance. At a junction, energy is conserved: heat rates arriving and leaving balance when there is no storage or generation at that junction. If two paths connect the same hot and cold temperatures, they are in parallel: rates add, so 1/Req = 1/R1 + 1/R2. These rules are useful only after drawing the path and checking that the assumptions of steady state and common series rate or common parallel temperature difference actually apply.

As a worked example, take the slab from the practice calculation: L = 0.020 m, k = 0.50 W/(m·K), and A = 0.10 m². Its conductive resistance is 0.020/(0.50×0.10) = 0.40 K/W. If one face convects to a fluid with h = 100 W/(m²·K) over the same area, the convective resistance is 1/(100×0.10) = 0.10 K/W. In this simple series model Rtotal = 0.50 K/W. With a 50 K difference between the fluid and the far-side reference, q̇ = 50/0.50 = 100 W. Forty kelvin falls across the slab and ten kelvin across the convective film, since each drop is q̇R. This recovers the existing series-resistance practice item and makes clear that the resistance values depend on the stated area and boundary model.

The same bookkeeping pattern appears in mass transfer, but the constitutive laws and units differ. A diffusive molar flux is driven by a concentration gradient; an interfacial film model often uses a mass-transfer coefficient times a concentration difference. A mass-transfer resistance is not measured in K/W, and a thermal resistance cannot be substituted for it numerically. Heat and solute can also interact: temperature changes diffusivity, viscosity, reaction rates, and solubility; evaporation or a reaction can couple latent or chemical energy to species transport. A model that treats the fields independently assumes those feedbacks are negligible over the question's conditions.

## Transient response and the lumped-capacitance test

Resistance networks above describe steady rates. A solid also stores energy, so during warming or cooling its temperature changes with time. The lumped-capacitance model replaces the body's internal temperature field by one uniform temperature T(t). For a body of density ρ, specific heat capacity cp, and volume V exchanging heat by convection over area As with a fluid held at T∞, an energy balance gives ρcpV dT/dt = hAs(T∞−T), when properties are constant and there is no internal heat generation or phase change. If T∞ and h stay constant, the solution is T(t)−T∞ = [Ti−T∞] exp(−t/τ), where Ti is the initial temperature and τ = ρcpV/(hAs). A larger thermal mass slows the response; more area or stronger convection speeds it up.

The uniform-temperature assumption needs a check. The Biot number compares internal conduction resistance with external convection resistance: Bi = hLc/k, using the characteristic length Lc = V/As. A small Bi means conduction inside the solid is relatively effective at smoothing temperature, compared with the surface exchange. Bi below about 0.1 is a common screening criterion for lumped analysis, not a guarantee independent of shape, boundary conditions, or required accuracy. For a plane slab exposed on both broad faces, V/As is half the full thickness; using the wrong length changes Bi by a factor of two. If Bi is not small, appreciable internal gradients may control the transient and a spatial heat equation or a validated transient solution is needed. A Fourier number Fo = αt/Lc², with thermal diffusivity α = k/(ρcp), helps describe how far internal diffusion has progressed over a time t; it does not erase the boundary-condition assumptions.

Radiation can be included in an effective surface coefficient only by linearizing it over the temperature range of interest. Internal generation, strong temperature-dependent properties, nonuniform fluid temperature, changing phase, or a body with large internal gradients can invalidate the simple exponential solution. In those cases write an energy balance that includes the missing terms and choose a model capable of representing the relevant variation. A neat resistance network is not evidence that the physical assumptions hold.

## A practical model check

Before calculating, draw the path from the warmer reservoir to the cooler one. Mark each solid, fluid, interface, and radiation gap; choose a consistent area and identify whether each segment is in series or parallel. State whether the calculation is steady or transient, whether there is internal generation, and which temperatures are surface values versus bulk-fluid values. Check units: thermal resistance is K/W, h is W/(m²·K), heat rate is W, and thermal capacity ρcpV is J/K. For a transient solid, compute Bi before assuming one temperature represents the whole body. Compare competing rates or dimensionless groups before deleting a mechanism, and revisit the model if the predicted temperature or property change would invalidate the assumed constant coefficients. These checks make the calculation interpretable; they do not turn first-order estimates into a validated design method.
