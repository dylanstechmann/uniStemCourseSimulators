# Thermodynamics & Transport scope crosswalk

This crosswalk compares the local package, version 0.7.0, with two openly published undergraduate courses: MIT OpenCourseWare 20.110J Thermodynamics of Biomolecular Systems, Fall 2005 ([calendar](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/20-110j-thermodynamics-of-biomolecular-systems-fall-2005/pages/calendar/), 36 lectures), and the fluids and thermodynamics lectures of Open Yale Courses PHYS 200 Fundamentals of Physics I, taught by Ramamurti Shankar and recorded in Fall 2006 ([course page](https://oyc.yale.edu/physics/phys-200), Lectures 20–24). It was compiled with AI assistance on 2026-10-28 from each comparator's public lecture titles, from the overviews and chapter titles of Yale Lectures 20–24, and from the local readings and practice. It is an agent-run comparison, not a review by the owner or by a thermodynamics instructor. It does not compare lecture content, problem sets, exams or workload, and topic alignment establishes no equivalence, credit or endorsement. The comparison of the package with MIT 20.330J Fields, Forces and Flows in Biological Systems is recorded in the [scope audit](../../../docs/COURSE_SCOPE_AUDIT.md) and is not repeated here.

Two scope notes apply. MIT 20.110J is a physical-chemistry style course on biomolecular systems, so its later lectures on polymers, DNA hybridization and cooperativity are specialties beyond a bioengineering transport package, and the crosswalk records them as such. PHYS 200 is an introductory physics course in which fluids fill Lecture 20 and thermodynamics Lectures 21–24. Status labels: **covered** means a local reading develops the topic with a worked example and practice; **shallow** means a local reading teaches part of the topic; **absent** means no local instruction; **local extension** means the package teaches a topic neither comparator names in its titles. Local numbers are lesson IDs (transport-N), and each links to its reading.

## What 0.7.0 changed

Before 0.7.0 the package balanced energy for flowing control volumes (lessons 1 and 16) and entropy for closed systems and steady flow (lesson 14), but no reading, practice item or card used the terms "state function", "internal energy", "ideal gas", "Carnot" or "heat engine". Both comparators teach the closed-system foundation: 20.110J in Lectures 1–4 and Yale PHYS 200 in Lectures 21–23. A new week-1 reading, lesson 23, precedes the entropy lesson. It defines state and path functions, applies ΔU = Q − W with the course's sign convention, develops the ideal gas, C_v, C_p and γ, and works the isothermal, adiabatic, isobaric and isochoric processes of one gas with numbers. It also compares a reversible with an irreversible expansion between the same states and connects the lost work to the entropy generated. It adds three objectives, 13 practice items and four retrieval cards.

## Remaining gaps

- Heat engines and the Carnot cycle, named by Yale PHYS 200 Lectures 23–24 and by no MIT 20.110J title.
- Thermochemistry (MIT Lecture 5) and the Joule–Thomson experiment (MIT Lecture 3).
- Statistical thermodynamics: the Boltzmann distribution, occupation of states and the third law (MIT Lectures 13–16; Yale Lectures 22 and 24).
- Single-component phase equilibria, Clausius–Clapeyron and latent heat (MIT Lectures 17–18; Yale Lecture 21).
- Solvation and the colligative properties other than osmotic pressure (MIT Lecture 21), and electrolyte solutions and the Debye length (MIT Lectures 27–28).
- Archimedes' principle and the hydraulic press (Yale Lecture 20).
- The MIT biomolecular specialties of Lectures 23–26 and 29–35, which are beyond this package's boundary.
- Graded homework and exams, measured workload, and review by a qualified person.
