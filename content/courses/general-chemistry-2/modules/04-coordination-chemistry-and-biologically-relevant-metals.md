# Coordination chemistry and biologically relevant metals

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:

1. Identify a metal center and its donor atoms, and explain how its protein environment can alter redox behavior.
2. Explain how ligand-field strength changes d-orbital splitting and can affect spin, color, and redox behavior.

## Coordination complexes: a metal and its ligands

A coordination complex contains a central metal atom or ion bonded to surrounding **ligands**. A ligand is a molecule or ion with one or more electron pairs it can donate to the metal center. This donor–acceptor description is often represented as a coordinate covalent bond. Water, ammonia, chloride, and many biological groups can act as ligands, although the strength and geometry of their interactions differ.

The **coordination sphere** contains the central metal and its attached ligands. In a formula such as [Co(NH₃)₅Cl]Cl₂, the square brackets separate the complex ion from counterions outside it. The coordination number counts the donor atoms directly attached to the metal, not the number of ligand molecules and not the charge on the complex. Establish the coordination sphere first; then charge bookkeeping can help assign a formal oxidation state. In the example, the complex ion has charge +2, ammonia is neutral, and the coordinated chloride is −1. If cobalt's oxidation state is x, then x + 5(0) − 1 = +2, so x = +3. The two external chloride ions balance the complex's positive charge but do not count as coordinated donors.

An oxidation state is an accounting convention that helps organize electron bookkeeping. It is not a direct measurement of localized charge on the metal: covalent metal–ligand bonding distributes electron density across the complex. Keep formal oxidation state, coordination number, ligand count, and total complex charge distinct.

## Count donor atoms and recognize denticity

A **monodentate** ligand attaches through one donor atom. A **polydentate** ligand attaches through more than one donor atom; bidentate means two. The number of ligand molecules can therefore differ from the coordination number. For example, a constructed octahedral complex [M(en)₂Cl₂] contains two bidentate ethylenediamine ligands (en) and two monodentate chloride ligands. The donor count is 2×2 + 2×1 = 6, so the metal has coordination number six. Counting only four ligand molecules would give the wrong result.

Heme illustrates the idea in a biological setting: its porphyrin framework supplies four nitrogen donor atoms to iron, while the surrounding protein can provide additional axial contacts. This structural description identifies a coordination sphere. It does not by itself tell us the metal's redox potential, whether oxygen binds under a particular condition, or how rapidly a ligand exchanges. Those require electronic, thermodynamic, or kinetic evidence beyond a donor count.

## Coordination number constrains geometry, but may not determine it

Many complexes with coordination number six are approximately octahedral. Four-coordinate complexes commonly adopt either tetrahedral or square-planar geometries; the coordination number alone does not choose between them. The metal's electron configuration, ligand interactions, geometry-dependent orbital energies, and steric constraints help determine which arrangement is favored. Distorted and less common geometries also occur, especially in proteins where the folded structure constrains the available positions.

This is a useful point of contrast with simple main-group VSEPR problems: coordination number is a donor count, while the complex's three-dimensional geometry is a structural question. A drawing or formula that gives only the count of ligands does not always identify all donor positions or stereochemistry. Evidence such as spectroscopy, diffraction, or a well-supported structural model may be needed to resolve alternatives.

## Ligand fields and electronic behavior

In an isolated metal ion, the five d orbitals are degenerate in the simplest model. Nearby ligands create an electric field that splits their energies. For an ideal octahedral arrangement, three lower-energy orbitals are grouped as t₂g and two higher-energy orbitals as e_g; the separation is called Δₒ. This crystal-field picture is a simplified model of metal–ligand interactions, but it helps explain selected electronic and magnetic observations.

Ligands differ in how strongly they split the d-orbital energies. Replacing weak-field ligands with strong-field ligands generally increases Δₒ in the same idealized geometry. Whether electrons pair in the lower set or occupy higher orbitals depends on both the splitting and the cost of pairing, as well as the metal's d-electron count. Thus a stronger field can favor a low-spin arrangement for suitable complexes, but the label “strong field” alone does not establish the spin state for every metal and geometry.

Changes among d-orbital energy levels can allow a complex to absorb visible light; the observed color often reflects which wavelengths remain or are transmitted. Some complexes have no d–d transition in the visible range or show color dominated by other transitions, so not every metal complex is vividly colored. Spectra and magnetic measurements provide evidence about electronic structure; a color name alone does not uniquely determine geometry, oxidation state, or spin.

The week-12 practice item asks what a strong-field substitution tends to do in an octahedral complex. The supported general answer is that it increases the splitting and can favor low spin for appropriate electron counts, while also changing possible absorption and redox behavior. It does not follow that every such change has one fixed magnitude or direction. ## Why protein environments can tune metal behavior

The same metal ion can have different behavior in different proteins because its immediate environment differs. Donor identity and arrangement affect the electronic energies of metal and ligand states; local electrostatics and hydrogen bonding can stabilize one oxidation state more than another; solvent exposure, protonation, and substrate access can alter which reaction paths are available. A folded protein positions groups around a metal, making it possible for the coordination sphere to support one function in one context and another function elsewhere.

For a redox-active center, these effects can shift the relative free energies of oxidized and reduced states and therefore influence its reduction potential. They can also affect reaction rates through substrate binding, reorganization, or access. These are related but distinct effects: an equilibrium or potential describes thermodynamic preference, while a rate reports how rapidly a process occurs. A ligand-field diagram alone does not yield a measured biological potential, and a protein sequence alone does not prove the complete mechanism.

Biological metal sites also include metals whose main roles are structural or Lewis-acid catalysis rather than reversible electron transfer. Zinc, for instance, often remains in one formal oxidation state while its ligands position a substrate or stabilize charge. It would be a mistake to assume every metal-containing protein is a redox carrier. Conversely, the formal oxidation state does not reveal how much electron density is shared with the protein ligands.

## Common reasoning checks

- Count coordinated donor atoms, including each donor from a multidentate ligand; exclude counterions outside the coordination sphere.
- Use the complex-ion charge and ligand charges to assign a formal metal oxidation state, then label it as electron bookkeeping rather than a direct charge measurement.
- Do not infer a unique geometry from coordination number four alone; tetrahedral and square-planar examples both exist.
- Connect ligand-field strength to orbital splitting, but use d-electron count and pairing energy before predicting spin.
- Treat color, redox potential, and reaction rate as different observations. Ligands and protein context can affect all three, but one measurement does not determine the others.
- Separate the general concept that a protein environment tunes metal behavior from a claim about any particular real protein or treatment.
