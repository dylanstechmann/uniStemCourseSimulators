# Calculus II scope crosswalk

This crosswalk compares the local package, version 0.7.0, with two openly published undergraduate single-variable calculus courses: MIT OpenCourseWare 18.01SC Single Variable Calculus, Fall 2010 ([syllabus](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/18-01sc-single-variable-calculus-fall-2010/pages/syllabus/), Units 3–5), and Harvard Math 1b Introduction to Functions and Calculus II and Differential Equations, taught by Robin Gottlieb in Spring 2004 ([syllabus](https://abel.math.harvard.edu/archive/1b_spring_04/syllabus.html)). It was compiled with AI assistance on 2026-10-28 from each comparator's public unit, part, session and weekly-block titles and from the local readings and practice. It is an agent-run comparison, not a review by the owner or by a mathematics instructor. It does not compare lecture content, problem sets, exams or workload, and topic alignment establishes no equivalence, credit or endorsement.

Two scope notes apply. MIT 18.01SC covers all of single-variable calculus, and the local package splits that sequence between Calculus I and Calculus II, so only 18.01SC's integration, series and applications units (Units 3 through 5) bear on this package. Harvard Math 1b is a second-semester course for students who already know basic calculus, and it spends its last third on differential equations, which the local catalog teaches in the Differential Equations course. Status labels: **covered** means a local reading develops the topic with a worked example and practice; **absent** means no local instruction; **elsewhere** means another local course teaches it. Local numbers are lesson IDs (calculus-2-N), and each links to its reading.

## What 0.7.0 changed

Averages and probability were the one strand that both comparators teach and the package lacked. 18.01SC gives Unit 3 Part C to integrals and averages, weighted averages, probability and the bell curve, and Harvard's integration block has average value, density and slicing, and probability. The new week-6 reading follows the accumulation lesson and uses the improper-integrals lesson. It develops the average value and the mean value theorem for integrals, totals from linear and population densities, density-weighted averages, probability densities with normalization, interval and tail probabilities, the mean and the median, the way an improper integral decides whether a density's total and mean are finite, and the bell curve as a case for numerical integration. It adds three objectives, 13 practice items and four retrieval cards.
