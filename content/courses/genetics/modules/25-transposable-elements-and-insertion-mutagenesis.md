# Transposable elements and insertion mutagenesis

Some DNA moves. Insertion sequences and their composite transposons hop between genomic sites, disrupt genes when they land, and mark their own exits when they leave. This original reading adds transposition mechanics, insertion mutagenesis and revertant analysis to the existing microbial-exchange and mapping material. Every element, operon, frequency and count here is an invented teaching parameter, not a real mobile element, a wet-lab protocol or a measured strain.

## An insertion sequence is a small mobile cassette

Stipulate a compact insertion sequence: a transposase coding region bracketed at both ends by inverted repeats, the DNA sites the element's own protein recognizes. The bookkeeping used here counts two inverted-repeat pairs per element, one pair at each end. Two such cassettes flanking an ordinary marker gene make a composite transposon: the two ends move as one unit and drag the middle along. The marker is incidental to the mechanics; it just makes movement easy to count on a selective plate. The lesson's counts are structural, not observed: two inverted-repeat pairs per insertion sequence, and two insertion-sequence copies in the composite.

## Two mechanisms, two copy-number signatures

A cut-and-paste element excises from its donor site and reinserts elsewhere: the element's copy number is unchanged, and the donor site is left to be repaired. A replicative element instead copies itself into a new site while the original stays behind, often through a briefly joined cointegrate intermediate: the copy number rises by one per event. Stipulated arithmetic: starting from one genomic copy, one replicative event leaves two copies, while one cut-and-paste event still leaves one. The copy-number signature is therefore evidence about the mechanism; a hybridization-style count of element bands distinguishes the two stories without watching any event happen. The same logic that separated recombination from mutation in the earlier microbial lessons applies here: choose the model that the band count can test, and state what the count cannot decide.

## Insertion mutagenesis and polarity

An element landing inside a coding region destroys that gene's function, but in an operon the damage can reach further than the landing site. Stipulate a three-gene operon transcribed as one messenger, and an element carrying a transcription terminator that inserts into gene 2. Downstream gene 3 expression collapses to 2.0 percent of wild type, while upstream gene 1 expression stays at 100 percent. The insertion is polar: it does not touch gene 3, yet gene 3's product disappears with it. Classifying an insertion series therefore needs two kinds of evidence — which gene's coding region is disrupted, and what happens to every downstream transcript — and a polar misannotation is a real mapping error, not a stylistic one. The direct lesion and the collateral collapse are different facts, and a complementing plasmid that rescues the phenotype does not say which one occurred.

## Counting insertion mutants

Stipulate a culture of one billion cells and a transposition frequency of 2.0×10⁻⁷ events per cell: expect about 200 independent insertion mutants. Spread over 50 equally sized target genes, the expected count is 4.0 mutants per gene, and the Poisson chance that a given gene still has zero insertion mutants is e⁻⁴, about 1.83 percent. The same arithmetic that drives the fluctuation test in the mutation lesson reappears here: expected counts per gene are small enough that absence of evidence is a statement about sampling, not about the gene's immunity. A saturated series is a claim about coverage, and coverage is a number, not an adjective.

## Reversion: excision or suppression

A revertant colony does not announce its mechanism. Precise excision removes the element and restores the wild-type sequence, at a stipulated one revertant per billion progeny. A second-site suppressor instead patches the phenotype elsewhere — at a stipulated 0.5 per million progeny, which is 500 per billion and therefore 500 times more common. The ratio is the diagnosis: precise excision restores the original sequence, while suppression leaves the element in place and changes something else. Polymerase or product evidence separates them; phenotype alone cannot, and a revertant that still carries the element is a suppressor until proved otherwise.

## Common mistakes

Do not call every insertion polar: polarity requires downstream transcription to be affected, not just the landing gene. Do not infer mechanism from phenotype: a revertant may be suppression rather than excision. Do not read expected counts as guaranteed counts; 200 expected mutants does not promise 200 observed. Do not treat a complementing plasmid as a map: rescue says the phenotype is rescuable, not which lesion caused it.

## Limits of this lesson

Every element, operon and frequency is synthetic. Nothing here predicts the behavior of any real transposon, authorizes genome manipulation, or constitutes biological validation. The package remains partial, unreviewed and formative-only.
