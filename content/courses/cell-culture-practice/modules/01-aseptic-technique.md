# Aseptic technique and a medium change with hidden contamination

A cell culture can look normal and still be ruined. Contamination may arrive from a medium lot, a dirty cabinet surface, a reused pipette, a cap placed face down, or a second cell line handled in the same cabinet. Some contaminants make the medium turbid within days; mollicutes can multiply without visible turbidity and can pass the 0.22 µm filter that routinely sterilizes medium. This lesson treats aseptic technique as a set of preventive decisions and contamination control as an evidence problem. You rehearse the decisions in the [aseptic medium-change bench simulation](#/bench/aseptic-medium-change-with-hidden-contamination), which uses a generic adherent mammalian line and synthetic hidden events.

This is lesson 1 of the outlined cell-culture layer. Lessons 1–6 of the outlined cell-culture layer are written and available; only lessons 7–8 (human pluripotent stem cells and records) remain deferred. Aseptic technique is the prerequisite for every later culture procedure.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:

1. Explain why a Class II cabinet, its airflow and a clean-to-dirty layout protect a culture, and identify the pre-work checks needed before a medium change.
2. Apply sterile-to-sterile manipulation rules during a medium change: one cell line, fresh pipettes, correct closure handling, side approach, slow motion and no talking over exposed product.
3. Calculate and interpret a sterility-control incubation schedule within 20–35 °C, including the dual-temperature requirement.
4. Distinguish visible turbidity, standard sterility incubation and dedicated mycoplasma testing, and explain why 0.22 µm filtration does not prove that mollicutes are absent.
5. Evaluate contamination responses, including containment, waste decontamination, and why antibiotics are not a rescue for aseptic failure.

## What the cabinet does and does not do

A Class II biological safety cabinet is the accepted primary containment for culturing mammalian cells. BMBL, 6th edition, says that human and other primate cells should be treated as potentially infectious and handled at a minimum with BSL-2 practices, engineering controls and facilities, and that using a biological safety cabinet for culturing activities is the universally accepted best practice (current Appendix H, printed p. 473). A risk assessment must come first, especially for human, primate or primary material (printed p. 471).

The cabinet's product protection depends on HEPA-filtered unidirectional airflow. FDA's aseptic-processing guidance describes the critical zone as ISO 5 (Class 100) and gives a typical vertical unidirectional velocity of 0.45 m/s ±20% in the working area (printed p. 6). That airflow is easily disturbed. Interventions increase contamination risk; contact sterile materials only with sterile instruments or surfaces; sanitize or change gloves repeatedly; move slowly; keep your body out of the airflow path; and avoid unnecessary motion or talking near exposed product (printed pp. 13–14). Geraghty et al. add cell-culture-specific rules: swab the cabinet and items with 70% alcohol before work, do not make rapid movements, manipulate fluids slowly, never obstruct the grilles or front opening, do not overcrowd the cabinet, organize the area so sterile reagents and cultures do not contact contaminants, clean after each session, and do not use a Bunsen burner in a cabinet (§3.4.1).

A UV lamp may be installed, but its effectiveness is limited and shadowed. It does not replace chemical surface disinfection (§3.4.1). FDA likewise requires sterile disinfectants and adequate contact time, and notes that 70% isopropyl alcohol is not sporicidal (printed p. 34).

## Pre-work checks

Before opening anything, inspect the supplies and the cultures:

- Check the cabinet's current field-test label and operating state. Cabinet field testing and spill response are practised in the [Class II cabinet simulation](#/bench/class-ii-bsc-session-with-spill); this lesson assumes the cabinet has passed its current field test and is running correctly.
- Read every medium label: lot, expiry, storage, filter specification and whether the bottle belongs to this cell line. An expired lot is not a judgement call; do not use it.
- Check flask labels and appearance. A visibly turbid flask is not "probably fine"; keep it closed, contain it and dispose of it separately.
- Confirm that only one cell line will enter the cabinet. Geraghty et al. identify having two cell lines in a cabinet at the same time as a cause of cross-contamination and misidentification (§1.5) and recommend working with only one culture at a time, using dedicated media and reagents (§4.2.3).
- Surface-disinfect every item before it enters. Items brought into a cabinet are clean but not sterile (§3.4.1).

## Clean-to-dirty layout

A useful teaching layout has three zones:

| Zone | Contents | Reason |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Clean side | Fresh medium, sterile pipettes, pipette aid, sterility-control broth | These must not contact dirty items or waste |
| Work centre | Target culture flask | The open culture stays in the protected critical zone |
| Dirty side | Visibly turbid flask, spent-medium waste container | Contaminated or spent material is kept away from clean supplies |

Do not stack items, block the front opening or obstruct a grille. Keep only the materials needed for the immediate step inside. The bench simulation enforces one medium or waste bottle at a time as a teaching rule; real procedures may allow a carefully arranged set of bottles, but crowding and confusion are the risks being avoided.

## Sterile-to-sterile manipulation

The core rule is that a sterile surface may touch only a sterile surface. A cap placed face down picks up contaminants from the surface and then deposits them in the bottle or flask when replaced. Hold closures above the opening or place them upright on a disinfected surface; never place them face down (FDA, printed p. 13; Geraghty et al., §3.4.1).

During each transfer:

- Use a fresh sterile pipette for every flask, bottle and sterility-control sample. Reused pipettes are a common cross-contamination route (Geraghty et al., §§1.5 and 4.2.2).
- Approach from the side, not over an open flask or bottle (FDA, printed p. 14).
- Move slowly and gently; use a pipetting aid to avoid aerosols (Geraghty et al., §3.4.1).
- Do not talk, cough or make rapid movements over exposed product (FDA, printed pp. 13–14).
- Sanitize gloves with sterile alcohol before opening sterile items and again after handling dirty or contaminated material (FDA, printed p. 13).

## A medium change in order

**Worked example 1: layout and sequence for one T-75 flask.** You have one target flask, one visibly turbid flask from the same line, two fresh-medium lots, sterile pipettes, a waste container and sterility-control broth. Lot A expires in one month; lot B expired last week.

1. Inspect everything, wipe the empty interior with sterile 70% alcohol, allow contact time and sanitize gloves.
2. Load the target flask in the work centre, the turbid flask and waste container on the dirty side, and clean supplies on the clean side. Wipe each item first.
3. Inspect both flasks while closed. Open only the target flask.
4. Remove spent medium with a fresh pipette, using a side approach and slow motion. Draw a spent-medium sterility control if your procedure uses one.
5. Close the target flask, decontaminate the waste and remove it before bringing in fresh medium. This keeps one bottle inside at a time and prevents the clean medium bottle from sitting beside collected waste.
6. Load lot A, the in-date lot. Open it and the target flask, add the laboratory's working volume—the simulation uses 10–15 mL for a T-75—and close both.
7. Draw a fresh-medium sterility control, remove the medium bottle, submit a dedicated mycoplasma test and incubate the controls.
8. Dispose of the turbid flask sealed and decontaminated, sanitize gloves, remove the remaining items and give the empty cabinet a final sterile-alcohol wipe.

The expired lot is not used even if it looks clear. The turbid flask is never opened in the clean workflow. The target culture and turbid flask may carry the same cell-line label, so one cell line is still present; a different line must not be brought in "just for a minute".

## Contamination evidence

Visible turbidity is useful but late and incomplete. Geraghty et al. state that mycoplasma can grow to very high titres without producing turbidity or obvious symptoms, that mycoplasmas are about 0.15–0.3 µm and pleomorphic, that they pass standard 0.22 µm bacteriological filters, and that 0.1 µm filters are required for sterilization against them (§4.2). They also warn that mollicutes are often resistant to penicillin and streptomycin and that overreliance on antibiotics rather than aseptic technique can increase contamination (§§4.2 and 4.2.3). Routine dedicated testing is therefore the assured way to detect them (§4.2.4).

Standard sterility controls are different from a mycoplasma assay. FDA's process-simulation guidance requires media-fill units to be incubated for at least 14 days within 20–35 °C. When two temperatures are used, each must be held for at least 7 days, starting with the lower temperature (printed p. 25). Typical lower and higher ranges for fungal and bacterial recovery are 20–25 °C and 30–35 °C (printed p. 35). This lesson adapts that principle to sterility-control samples drawn from spent and fresh medium.

**Worked example 2: interpreting three results.** A medium change is performed correctly, but the hidden scenario can still contaminate the culture.

| Observation | Interpretation |
| --- | --- |
| Fresh-medium control turbid after 14 valid days; culture currently clear | The medium lot, sampling step or culture may be contaminated. Do not treat the clear appearance as proof of sterility; quarantine or discard according to your procedure and investigate the lot. |
| Culture clear; dedicated mycoplasma assay positive | Mollicutes are present despite the lack of turbidity. Antibiotics do not make the culture sterile; follow your laboratory's contamination and decontamination procedure. |
| Culture turbid after a reused pipette, face-down cap or dirty cabinet surface | A technique failure is the most likely source. Contain and dispose of the culture, decontaminate waste and the cabinet, and review the step that broke sterile-to-sterile contact. |

A valid incubation schedule is 7 days at 22 °C followed by 7 days at 32 °C: total 14 days, at least 7 at each temperature, lower first. Ten days at 22 °C alone is too short and one-temperature. Seven days at 32 °C followed by 7 days at 22 °C has the order reversed. Seven days at 22 °C followed by 7 days at 37 °C is outside the 20–35 °C media-fill range.

## Antibiotics, containment and waste

Adding penicillin-streptomycin after a suspected contamination is not a rescue. It may delay or mask visible growth, but it does not sterilize the culture, does not reliably eliminate mollicutes, and can hide a problem while the cells' behaviour changes. Use antibiotics only where your procedure justifies them, not to cover an aseptic error.

A visibly contaminated flask should remain closed, be contained and be disposed of appropriately (Geraghty et al., §3.4.2). BMBL requires all waste culture material to be decontaminated before disposal (current Appendix H, printed p. 473). After the session, remove every item, decontaminate surfaces and waste, and wipe the empty cabinet with an appropriate disinfectant (§3.4.1).

## Practise in the bench simulation

Open the [aseptic medium-change bench simulation](#/bench/aseptic-medium-change-with-hidden-contamination) and work through it as a formative exercise:

1. Inspect the supplies and reject the expired medium lot.
2. Disinfect the empty interior, sanitize gloves, and load only the needed items in a clean-to-dirty arrangement.
3. Keep the turbid flask closed, remove spent medium, and decontaminate and remove waste before loading fresh medium.
4. Add 10–15 mL from the in-date lot using a fresh pipette, side approach, slow motion and no talking.
5. Draw spent- and fresh-medium sterility controls, submit a dedicated mycoplasma test, and incubate 7 days at 20–25 °C followed by 7 days at 30–35 °C.
6. Dispose of the turbid flask sealed and decontaminated, remove every item, finish with a sterile-alcohol wipe and read the debrief.

The simulation has 25 checkpoints, from supplies inspection through incubation, containment and final cleaning. Its hidden events include a dirty cabinet interior, an expired lot, a visibly culturable medium contaminant and a mollicute-contaminated lot. The probabilities, the T-75 volume, the clean-to-dirty zones, the one-bottle rule, the simplified mycoplasma assay and the visible-growth threshold are teaching choices, not laboratory acceptance criteria. Nothing is graded or recorded as a qualification.

## Common mistakes

- Treating a cabinet as sterile because it is running, or treating UV as a substitute for wiping.
- Bringing two cell lines, unnecessary bottles or unwiped items into the cabinet.
- Placing a cap face down, reaching over an open flask, moving rapidly or talking near exposed product.
- Reusing one pipette between the turbid flask, target flask, waste and fresh medium.
- Using an expired medium lot because it looks clear.
- Adding antibiotics to mask a suspected contamination.
- Reading a clear culture as proof that mollicutes are absent, or reading a short or wrong-order sterility incubation as a negative result.
- Removing waste or a turbid flask without decontamination and sealed containment.
