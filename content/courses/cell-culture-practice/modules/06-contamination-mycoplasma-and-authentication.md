# Contamination, mycoplasma and cell-line authentication

**Status:** Original, unreviewed formative instruction for outline lesson 6 of the partial Cell Culture Practice package. All organisms, cultures, observations, profiles, incidents and source records are synthetic; the simulation rehearses evidence decisions, not physical technique, organism identification or laboratory validation.

A culture can be microbially contaminated, misidentified, both, or neither. Appearance alone cannot separate those possibilities. This lesson joins visible contamination clues to quarantine and disposal, invisible mycoplasma evidence, short tandem repeat (STR) authentication and an incident/source handoff. Its [contamination-diagnosis bench simulation](#/bench/diagnose-a-contaminated-culture-from-clues) uses five anonymous synthetic flasks and no real cell-line or donor data.

This is lesson 6 of the outlined cell-culture layer, scheduled as week 6 after cryopreservation in week 5. Lesson 1 supplies the aseptic-route and containment prerequisites. This lesson does not establish competency, physical skill, institutional authorisation, diagnostic validity or a validated rescue protocol. Local risk assessment, responsible laboratory staff and current institutional procedures govern real incidents.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:

1. Recognise visible bacterial and fungal clue patterns and connect contamination response to plausible routes without claiming species identification from appearance.
2. Explain why mycoplasma can remain invisible and compare culture, nucleic-acid amplification and DNA-stain evidence, including their respective limits and controls.
3. Apply quarantine before identification, choose discard versus a tightly bounded rescue referral, reject antibiotic masking and prepare a complete incident handoff.
4. Explain HeLa-era cross-contamination context and interpret synthetic STR loci and allele calls, distinguishing match evidence from mismatch and from proof of purity or phenotype.
5. Plan incoming-line quarantine, contamination and identity testing, and trace shared medium, equipment, handling and receipt records as alternative source hypotheses.

## Visible clues are triggers, not a species name

Inspect closed vessels and their microscopy panels before opening or manipulating them. Compare medium clarity, indicator colour or measured pH, cell morphology and the presence of particles or structures with the line's prior records. Geraghty and colleagues describe increased turbidity and pH change as common bacterial or fungal warning signs. Many bacteria acidify phenol-red medium toward yellow; some fungi can move it alkaline toward pink. These are patterns, not universal colour rules.

In this lesson's synthetic panels, the **bacterial-pattern flask** has turbid medium, a pH shift toward acid and numerous small rod-shaped objects. The **fungal-pattern flask** has branching filaments and micro-colonies, with an alkaline indicator shift. A Gram-stain or fungal-subculture result can support the respective broad category, but neither the appearance nor these simplified results identifies a genus or species. Filaments should not be dismissed as debris merely because the medium remains clear, and turbidity alone does not distinguish bacteria from fungi.

Routes are investigated after containment. Shared medium or supplements, non-sterile closures and pipette tips, contact with work surfaces, aerosols or droplets, contaminated water pans or incubators, handling several lines together, and receipt of an infected culture can all connect otherwise separate events. A route is a hypothesis ranked by evidence: common lots, timestamps, vessel locations, handling records and test results. It is not established by choosing the most memorable recent event. Lesson 1's one-line, fresh-pipette, closure and clean-to-dirty controls remain the prevention baseline.

## Mycoplasma: clear medium is not negative evidence

Mycoplasmas and related mollicutes lack a rigid cell wall, can pass some filters used for bacterial control and may reach high concentrations without turbidity or an obvious change in cell appearance. Drexler and Uphoff review effects on metabolism, growth, macromolecular synthesis and experimental readouts. Consequently, “looks healthy” and “was filtered” do not answer the mycoplasma question. The simulator deliberately hides mycoplasma status until a dedicated test is ordered, including for a flask that looks entirely normal.

Three method families illustrate why the name of a test is not enough:

| Method | What the evidence means | Important limits to carry into interpretation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Selective broth/agar culture | Growth of mycoplasma colonies supports detection and preserves a culture-based reference approach. | Slow and specialist; handling positive cultures creates its own containment burden. Some strains grow poorly or not at all on cell-free media, so no growth is not universally exclusionary. Pseudocolonies and subjective reading also matter. |
| PCR or another nucleic-acid amplification test | Detection of targeted mycoplasma nucleic-acid sequences can be rapid and sensitive and does not require the organism to grow in culture. | Target coverage depends on assay design. Carry-over DNA can create false positives; inhibitors can create false negatives. Positive, negative and internal amplification controls must be valid. Residual DNA and viable organisms are not interchangeable claims. |
| Direct or indicator-cell DNA fluorochrome staining, such as DAPI/Hoechst approaches | An appropriate extranuclear fluorescence pattern can support contamination; indicator cells can amplify a signal. | The stain binds DNA rather than proving a mycoplasma species. Other DNA, background and cell nuclei complicate interpretation; direct staining can be insensitive and subjective. Indicator-cell versions take longer. Controls and experienced reading are necessary. |

No single simulated readout is a universal sterility certificate. A negative result applies to the specimen, target coverage, detection limit, sampling time and control validity of that assay. Drexler and Uphoff discuss PCR together with culture as useful complementary approaches. The simulator accepts PCR, culture or DNA-stain evidence for its narrow scenario, but a correct clear-flask disposition requires **dedicated mycoplasma evidence**; appearance, Gram stain, fungal subculture and STR cannot substitute. Its binary results do not reproduce real assay sensitivity, species coverage or control failure rates.

## Quarantine precedes identification

Stop routine passage and experimental use. Keep vessels closed, separate the suspect and linked cultures from clean work, and preserve labels and records. Quarantine applies before a contaminant is named: waiting for a perfect identification while continuing to handle the culture creates an opportunity for spread. Incoming lines remain in quarantine until contamination status and identity evidence are reviewed. In the simulator, a test cannot be ordered until that flask has been inspected and quarantined.

Quarantine is a workflow state, not a particular shelf. Dedicated space, equipment and incubator arrangements are preferable where available; otherwise institutional procedures may use separation in time and sealed secondary containment. Record which other flasks, lots and workspaces are linked. Do not open a visibly contaminated flask in a clean workflow merely to obtain a more satisfying answer.

### Discard, replace or refer a rescue decision

For a routine, replaceable culture with visible bacterial or fungal contamination, the default is sealed, decontaminated disposal followed by replacement from a previously tested stock, plus cleaning and review of associated materials. Mycoplasma-positive cultures likewise need prompt containment and a disposal/replacement decision to prevent lateral spread. Authentication mismatch is different from microbial contamination: isolate and hold the line as misidentified, stop using its old data as though the label were true, and investigate rather than calling it “sterile but unusual.”

An irreplaceable line may justify referral to an experienced laboratory for a rescue assessment. That is not permission to add a routine antibiotic and continue. Eradication can fail, select resistance, injure or alter the cells, and require antibiotic-free follow-up and repeat testing. Rescue also does not repair a changed identity. The simulator offers no successful antibiotic-treatment pathway.

**Antibiotic masking is not rescue.** Penicillin-streptomycin can suppress some visible bacterial growth without sterilising the vessel, while mycoplasmas are unaffected by many routinely used antibiotics. A culture that becomes clearer after antibiotics can therefore remain contaminated and infectious to other cultures. In the simulator, an antibiotic attempt leaves the flask quarantined, produces no disposition and fails the no-masking checkpoint.

## Cross-contamination and the HeLa-era lesson

Cell cross-contamination is the introduction and eventual overgrowth of one cell line by another; mislabelling and clerical errors can produce a similar false identity without a living contaminant. The historical HeLa investigations showed that aggressively growing cells could replace cultures maintained under other names, undermining the assumed tissue origin of subsequent experiments. Geraghty and colleagues place those findings within a broader, continuing problem of cross-contamination and misidentification. The lesson is not “old records were unreliable”; it is that a plausible label, long use and familiar morphology do not authenticate a current stock.

The [ICLAC Register of Misidentified Cell Lines](https://iclac.org/databases/cross-contaminations/) is a current lookup resource for known problems, not a proof that an unlisted line is authentic. This lesson links to version 14, released 15 February 2026, under CC BY-NC-ND 4.0. **No register row, entry, count, table or downloadable dataset is copied or adapted here.** The simulator's labels and profiles are invented and are not drawn from that register.

## STR profiles: loci, alleles and bounded conclusions

A short tandem repeat **locus** is a named genomic location containing repeated sequence. An **allele call** reports the repeat designation observed at that locus. A human cell sample commonly has up to two autosomal allele calls at a locus; a homozygous call is one observed allele designation, not permission to count it twice when listing distinct alleles. A profile is a set of calls across multiple loci, usually supported by peak data and quality review rather than a manually typed table alone.

The current standard is **ANSI/ATCC ASN-0002-2022, Authentication of Human Cell Lines: Standardization of Short Tandem Repeat (STR) Profiling**. ATCC's public STR page describes multiplex amplification, allele scoring and 13 core STR markers; its matching page distinguishes high-probability relatedness from scores requiring further analysis. Those pages and the standard's official catalogue record are link-only references here. The full paid standard text was not reproduced or treated as public-domain content.

For the numeric exercises and simulator, a deliberately simple **classroom shared-call percentage** is defined as:

  100 × shared allele-call observations / total allele-call observations in the synthetic reference.

For repeated alleles, pair observations by their multiplicities rather than counting only whether an allele occurs anywhere. This arithmetic is an exercise in reading a tiny synthetic table, **not** the Tanabe or Masters algorithm, a universal threshold, or a substitute for ASN-0002 interpretation. Real interpretation must state the algorithm, compared loci, missing calls, reference provenance, mixtures, drift or microsatellite instability, and the standard's match categories.

**Worked example.** At four synthetic loci, the sample calls are `[8,8]`, `[10,11]`, `[12,14]`, `[7,9]`; the reference calls are `[8,9]`, `[10,11]`, `[12,12]`, `[7,9]`. Shared calls number 1 + 2 + 1 + 2 = **6**, out of eight reference observations, so the classroom percentage is 100 × 6/8 = **75.0%**. Counting three loci with any overlap instead of six shared calls answers a different question. In the simulator, the reference and result remain hidden until STR is ordered. A mismatch triggers an identity hold; a matching profile supports consistency with that reference only.

STR does not detect mycoplasma, prove sterility, establish species for a non-human line, guarantee tissue-specific behaviour or exclude every genetic change. A match to a misidentified reference can faithfully reproduce the original error. Amelogenin or other sex-linked information is not a general health or contamination test, and real human profiles require privacy and donor-reidentification controls. This package uses only synthetic locus names and numeric alleles with no donor identifiers.

## Incoming lines, source tracing and incident handoff

On receipt, preserve the source's accession and provenance records without assuming they make local testing unnecessary. Quarantine the line; inspect it; obtain appropriate microbial and mycoplasma evidence; authenticate a human line against a suitable reference; review controls and compatibility with the intended use; and release it only after documented review. Keep an early tested bank where possible. Periodic retesting and return to characterised stocks address later contamination and drift. Non-human lines need species- and context-appropriate authentication rather than automatic application of a human STR rule.

Each flask inspection reveals an original synthetic **linked-record bundle** in the inspected-records panel and readout. Use that bundle before source tracing: compare its lot, equipment, receipt/QC or handling clue with the explicitly listed competing hypotheses and the other revealed evidence. A complete reviewed bundle may support a no-route-identified hypothesis without proving that no unrecorded event occurred. Different records warrant different investigative leads; do not infer a route from appearance alone or treat a QC gap, shared lot, equipment flag or handling event as root-cause proof. The hidden condition assignment and expected source hypothesis remain unrevealed until the formative debrief, and the records never replace mycoplasma or STR evidence.

A useful incident handoff includes vessel identifiers, quarantine times, raw observations, tests and controls, STR reference provenance, dispositions, linked lots/equipment/lines, cleaning or decontamination requests, unresolved questions and the responsible recipient. It should distinguish observed results from inferred routes and prevent further experimental use while review is open. Do not insert donor names or unnecessary personal data into a teaching or incident record.

## Common mistakes

- Calling a turbid flask a named bacterial species, or dismissing branching filaments as ordinary debris.
- Continuing routine work while awaiting identification instead of quarantining first.
- Treating clear medium, 0.22 µm filtration, Gram stain or STR as a mycoplasma test.
- Reporting a negative PCR without checking target coverage and internal/positive/negative controls.
- Adding antibiotics until visible clues disappear and calling that sterilisation or rescue.
- Treating a long-used label, familiar morphology or absence from the ICLAC register as authentication.
- Counting loci with any shared allele instead of the requested shared allele calls.
- Treating an STR match as proof of purity, phenotype or correct reference provenance.
- Choosing a source story without lot, equipment, receipt and handling evidence.
